Some organizations are building real connections between the people running things and the people doing the work, and the effect is subtle but noticeable.

It starts small. A leader sits in on a service desk call. Not to audit, not to check a box, but to listen. They hear the customer's frustration about a system that works one way in the morning and another way by afternoon. They hear the agent explain the seventeen-step workaround they've developed. The leader goes back to the leadership meeting with that story still in their head.

This matters because distance creates a particular kind of problem. Not malice or neglect, just the simple physics of how information travels. A metric looks fine on a dashboard. A process looks efficient on paper. A tool seems to be doing its job. But the person closest to the work sees something different. They see the friction that doesn't show up in numbers. They see the creativity people employ to make things work. They see what actually matters to the person on the other end of the interaction.

Organizations that close this distance aren't doing it through big structural changes. They're doing it through genuine presence. A support leader who spends time actually supporting. An IT director who uses the systems their team builds. Someone with real decision-making power who makes time to understand the texture of frontline work, not just its shape.

What's interesting is how much better decisions become when they're made by people who understand both the numbers and the reality. Budget gets allocated differently when someone knows what the money actually does. Priorities shift when you understand what's truly creating friction versus what just looks broken. Process improvements actually improve things because they're solving real problems instead of theoretical ones.

It creates something else too. Frontline people feel differently when they know they're being heard by someone who can actually do something. Not thanked from a distance, not recognized in a company meeting, but genuinely understood and responded to. That changes what they bring to the work.

The organizations doing this well aren't pretending the hierarchy doesn't exist. They're not flattening things or creating false intimacy. They're just deciding that the distance between strategy and reality matters enough to cross it regularly. They're treating it as part of the actual job of leadership.

It's one of those things that feels obvious once you're around it. Of course leaders should understand the work their team does. Of course better information leads to better decisions. Of course people perform differently when they're genuinely understood. The thing worth noticing is how many organizations are making space for it, and how much better everything works when they do.
