The best digital workplace work happens when security and compliance sit at the same table from the very start.

There's something genuinely satisfying about watching a digital workplace team and a security function work together well. Not in the way where one group reluctantly agrees with the other, but in the real collaboration where both sides are genuinely trying to solve the same problem: how do we create an environment where people can work effectively while staying protected?

What makes this work is recognizing that security and compliance aren't separate from digital workplace design—they're part of it. When you're thinking about how employees access applications, how data flows through their day, what tools they need to do their best work, the security team isn't coming in afterward to say no to things. They're part of the conversation about what's possible and what's wise.

This changes everything about how decisions get made. Instead of a digital workplace team building something great and then having security raise concerns late, there's an actual dialogue. What are we trying to achieve? What are the real risks? What are the creative ways we can enable both? The conversations tend to focus on substance rather than just rules. Yes, compliance requirements exist and they matter deeply. But they're not obstacles to work around—they're constraints that help shape better solutions.

The teams that do this well tend to develop a shared language. They understand what each other cares about. Digital workplace leaders understand why security teams think in terms of zero-trust architecture or endpoint protection. Security leaders understand why employees need frictionless access to the tools they need, and they care about that too.

There's also something worth noting about how this builds trust across the organization. When employees see that security and digital workplace functions are working together thoughtfully, it changes how they experience both. They stop seeing compliance as punishment and start seeing it as part of how the organization actually cares about keeping them safe.

The practical stuff matters too. Documentation that both teams understand. Testing approaches that validate security without derailing productivity. Incident response plans that everyone's already thought through. These things only happen when both groups are genuinely invested in each other's success.

If you're leading a digital workplace function, there's real value in reaching out to your security and compliance colleagues not because you have to, but because you want their perspective early. They usually have fascinating insights about what's working well in your environment and where the real vulnerabilities might be hiding.

It's not complicated, but it is worth doing thoughtfully. The organizations that figure this out tend to have more secure environments and better employee experiences. Both things at once, which is exactly how it should work.
