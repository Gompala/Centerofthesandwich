There's a particular kind of friction that happens between vendors and customers that feels like it should be fixable but isn't. Everything's technically fine. The contract is being honored. But the relationship is rotting from the inside, and both sides are convinced the other one started it.

Often what's actually happening is one side has mentally downgraded the relationship from partnership to transaction. Not explicitly, not in a meeting where anyone announces it. Just gradually. And the thing about that shift is it's almost invisible until you're six months deep and wondering why your vendor stopped being proactive, or your customer stopped giving you useful feedback.

The customer side of this is particularly interesting because it often comes from a place that seems smart on the surface. Someone looks at the vendor relationship and thinks: "Okay, we have a contract. The vendor's job is X. I pay them for X. If they deliver X, we're done. That's the deal." It's clean. It's mechanical. It's also a way to make a functional partnership actively worse.

When you approach a vendor exclusively as a contract, you're basically saying "I will hold you exactly to the literal terms of what we agreed to, and nothing more will be expected or offered." But contracts are weird documents. They're written by lawyers trying to prevent worst-case scenarios, not by people trying to design great working relationships. They're the floor, not the ceiling. The moment you decide that's also the ceiling, you've created something that looks like a partnership but operates like a vending machine.

This matters because vendors adjust. They sense the shift. When a customer becomes purely transactional, a vendor's entire stance changes. They stop thinking about what would actually help you. They start thinking about what they're contractually required to do and nothing else. They respond to tickets instead of reaching out with ideas. They hit the terms you agreed to and then dial back the effort. Not because they're petty, but because they're matching energy. They're following the logic they're being presented with.

The toxic part accelerates from there. A transactional customer sees this reduced engagement and interprets it as laziness or indifference, which confirms their suspicion that the vendor was never worth real partnership anyway. The vendor sees a customer who has no interest in actually working together, just in extracting maximum value from a contract, and stops trying to deliver anything beyond the minimum. Both sides are right about what they're observing and completely wrong about what caused it.

What makes this different from a vendor who's actually failing is that everything's working. The service is being delivered. The contract's being met. But it feels hollow because it is hollow. There's no actual relationship underneath.

The strange part is this is completely reversible. You don't need a new vendor or a new contract. You just need someone to actually decide they want a working relationship instead of a vending machine. That looks like sharing what you're actually trying to do, not just what you need bought. It looks like giving useful feedback, not just complaint tickets. It looks like trusting that the vendor might have ideas worth hearing because they're actually trying to solve your problem, not just fulfill a line item.

Not every vendor deserves that. Some are genuinely bad at what they do or indifferent to customer outcomes. But plenty of partnerships that feel toxic are just relationships where someone decided to check out and then got surprised when the other person checked out too.
