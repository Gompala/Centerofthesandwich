The IT service owner is quietly one of the most important roles in how organizations actually work. Not because they have the most authority or the longest title, but because they hold something precious: the complete picture of how a service lives and breathes across an entire enterprise.

Think about what happens when someone in finance can't access their expense reporting system. That person needs help now. But the real work isn't reactive. It's the service owner, months earlier, understanding that expense reporting touches finance, procurement, HR, and accounting. It touches dozens of people's days. It has dependencies on infrastructure, security patches, database performance, and user training. And it has a business purpose that matters to the organization's rhythm.

This is the center of the sandwich. Not above, not below, but in the middle where everything meets.

What makes this role genuinely interesting is that it requires a particular kind of thinking. A service owner has to hold two truths at once: the technical reality of how systems work, and the human reality of what people need to do their jobs. They need to understand infrastructure deeply enough to make good decisions, but also listen carefully to what users are actually experiencing. They need to plan for the future while keeping today's service running well.

The best part is that this role brings coherence to something that could otherwise be scattered. Without a clear service owner, you get decisions made in isolation. You get teams optimizing for different things. You get surprises when changes in one area ripple somewhere unexpected. With a good service owner, there's someone thinking holistically about trade-offs, dependencies, and what matters most.

It's also a role that grows with the person. Someone starting out as a service owner learns which questions to ask, how to listen to different stakeholders, how to communicate about complex technical things in ways that actually make sense. They develop judgment about what risks matter and which ones don't. They build relationships across the organization that help them understand what's really going on.

In healthy organizations, service owners become the connective tissue. They're not managing up or down so much as across and through. They're the ones who can sit in a room and translate between infrastructure teams and business teams, between security and usability, between what's ideal and what's practical right now.

If you work in digital workplace functions, service ownership might be part of your day already, even if no one's called it that. If you're thinking about your team's structure or your own career path, it's worth recognizing what that role actually is: a chance to understand how something important really works, and to make it work better for the people who depend on it.
