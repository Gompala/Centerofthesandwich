There's this moment that happens in a lot of distributed teams where someone realizes they don't actually know what their coworkers do. Not in a surface way, but in the real way. They know the job title, they know the person shows up to meetings, but the actual texture of the work, the thinking behind it, the problems that keep someone up at night, the small wins nobody celebrates, that part is opaque.

This is the thing nobody mentions when they talk about remote culture. They talk about structured coffee chats and virtual happy hours and intentional ice breakers. They talk about transparency and documentation and over-communication. All of that stuff might be true, might even be necessary. But the teams that actually feel cohesive aren't the ones that engineered connection. They're the ones where connection happened as a side effect of how they work.

There's a difference between a team that has culture and a team that has decided to have culture. One feels like you're part of something. The other feels like you're participating in an initiative.

The teams that work tend to have something specific in common, though it doesn't look like much on paper. They have a place where actual thinking happens out loud. Sometimes that's a dedicated Slack channel where people share what they're wrestling with, not as a status update but as a genuine question. Sometimes it's a weekly call where there's actual time for someone to say "I'm not sure how to approach this" and have that land as an opening rather than a problem. Sometimes it's a document culture where people write things down in a way that invites reaction, not just consumption.

The key part is that it's real. It's not optimized for looking collaborative. It's collaboration that actually solves something.

This matters because distributed work has this way of making everything feel either very formal or very nothing. You're either in a structured meeting where everyone performs competence, or you're in a chat where everyone performs efficiency. There's not much space for the middle part where work is actually interesting.

The teams getting this right aren't doing anything exotic. They're usually just protecting some kind of unstructured thinking space where it's clear that being half-formed is okay. Where someone can say "here's what I'm seeing" and it doesn't immediately become a thing that needs to be resolved or actioned. Where the goal is understanding each other, not checking a box.

It also tends to happen when there's someone or a small group of someones who just do it. Who regularly ask questions that don't have obvious answers. Who share their reasoning, not just their conclusions. Who seem genuinely curious about how other people are approaching problems. That modeling matters more than any policy.

The reason this matters for how distributed teams actually function is that you can't build trust on structure alone. You need to know the people you work with, and knowing someone isn't about having their Slack status synced with your calendar. It's about having some sense of how they think, what they care about, where they get stuck, what they get excited about.

In an office, some of that just happens. You hear someone on a call and get context. You see someone working on something and ask about it. You build a picture of people over time without trying. In a distributed setup, none of that happens by accident.

But when teams figure out how to create the conditions for it anyway, something shifts. The work feels different. The collaboration doesn't require as much scaffolding. People actually want to talk to each other because they've built up some real sense of connection to how each other works.

That's the culture that actually holds in a distributed environment. Not the one that was designed. The one that emerged because the team made space for it.
