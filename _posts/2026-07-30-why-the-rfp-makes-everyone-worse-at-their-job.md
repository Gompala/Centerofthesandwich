There's a moment in every RFP process where someone on your team realizes they're basically writing fiction. Not intentionally. Not maliciously. But they're describing a service delivery model that looks good on paper, reads smoothly, answers the client's stated criteria perfectly, and bears maybe a 60 percent resemblance to what will actually happen when the contract starts.

The weird part? Everyone knows this is happening. The vendors know it. The procurement people reading the responses probably know it. And yet the whole apparatus just keeps grinding forward, rewarding the teams that can construct the most convincing narrative.

It's not that vendors are lying exactly. It's that the RFP format creates this funnel where execution complexity and real-world constraints have to be compressed into narrative paragraphs. You can't easily explain "we'll do this but honestly it depends on how messy your data is." You have to pick a lane and defend it. You have to make your process sound like it's been tested in a laboratory when really you're going to figure some of it out when you get in there.

And clients writing RFPs face their own version of this. They're trying to specify something that hasn't happened yet, with requirements that sometimes conflict, against criteria that measure how well a vendor responds rather than how well a vendor performs. So they write RFPs that test writing ability. It's what they can measure.

What gets lost in that exchange is the stuff that actually matters. How does this vendor handle the moment when the original plan stops working? How do they talk to you when something unexpected happens? What's their actual operating rhythm, not their prescribed one? These things don't translate well into evaluation matrices.

The RFP privileges clarity over accuracy, polish over honesty. A vendor who writes "We will implement a phased approach over six months with three gates and quarterly steering committees" scores higher than one who writes "We'll do this in phases but the actual timeline depends on what we find when we start digging into your infrastructure." Even if the second one is more honest about how this kind of work actually goes.

So you get these weird dynamics where the team that's best at proposal writing wins the contract, and then the actual delivery team is scrambling because the promises made on the page don't quite align with what's feasible. The client is frustrated because the service doesn't match the description. And everyone involved spends the early months of the engagement renegotiating the narrative they collectively agreed to.

There's probably no perfect solution here. Some structure is necessary. But the RFP as it stands optimizes for something that doesn't correlate with good outcomes. It's like hiring based on resume formatting. You might get a great employee that way, sure. But you're measuring the wrong thing.

The vendors who win are the ones with strong proposal teams. The vendors who deliver the best outcomes might be completely different organizations. And that mismatch sits at the center of a lot of disappointed clients and overworked vendor teams trying to make good on promises that were always going to be approximate.

It's a system that works efficiently if you don't care about the actual results. If you just care about having a defensible decision process, the RFP is perfect. You can point to the evaluation rubric, the scoring, the clear criteria. You picked the top-ranked vendor. What could be more fair than that? But fairness in the selection process and quality in the delivery process are not the same thing, and the RFP confuses the two.
