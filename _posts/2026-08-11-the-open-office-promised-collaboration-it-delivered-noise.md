There was a moment when open offices felt inevitable. The logic was clean: tear down walls, watch serendipitous collisions happen, suddenly your organization becomes Google. Except serendipitous collisions mostly meant constant interruptions, and nobody actually got better at their jobs. They just got better at wearing headphones.

Now we're at this weird inflection point where the open office isn't technically dead but nobody's defending it with straight faces anymore. It's become one of those things organizations quietly back away from without admitting they were wrong about it in the first place.

What happened is partly practical, partly cultural. The pandemic forced a hard reset on where work actually happens. Some people discovered they could focus better at home. Others realized they'd been commuting an hour each way to sit at a desk with no walls while someone microwaved fish three feet away. Companies that insisted on five days in the office started losing people to competitors who didn't. So they compromised with hybrid models, which sounds balanced in theory but mostly created logistics nightmares.

But the real shift happened at the level of how we think about work itself. Open offices were based on this theory that proximity equals productivity and creativity. That's technically possible, but it requires the right conditions and the right people, and most open offices just had... people. Regular people trying to do different kinds of work at different times. Some needed deep focus. Some needed to collaborate. Some just needed to not listen to Dave's entire phone call.

What's replacing it isn't actually one thing. Some companies are doing hybrid, which lets people choose where they're most effective for different types of work. Some are creating smaller teams with flexible spaces instead of one massive floor. Some are building actual quiet zones, which sounds absurdly basic but represents a fundamental acknowledgment that collaboration isn't the only thing that matters. The successful offices now are the ones that assume different people need different environments, sometimes on the same day.

Tools matter here too. When your team is scattered across time zones and work styles, you need something like Teams that actually works for async communication. The open office was partly a crutch for bad tooling. If you can't reliably collaborate remotely, you push everyone into the same room and hope it works out. Now that remote tooling isn't terrible anymore, you have options.

There's also been a quiet death of the "culture will just happen if we're all here" theory. Turns out culture gets built through actual intentional things: clear communication, shared values, psychological safety. You can have all of that in an open office or destroy it instantly. You can also build it in a fully remote team. The physical layout was never the variable that mattered as much as we thought.

So the open office plan didn't fail because offices are bad or collaboration is bad. It failed because it was a lazy solution to a complex problem. It assumed that the best way to make people work better together was to remove any choice in how they work at all. That particular wager lost.

What's interesting now is watching organizations actually think about space and work separately, rather than assuming they're the same thing. It's slower than "let's just remove the walls," but it produces something that actually works for more people, which turns out to be the whole point.
