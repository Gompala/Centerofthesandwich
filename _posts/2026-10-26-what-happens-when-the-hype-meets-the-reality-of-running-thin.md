An economist at Apollo recently noted something worth sitting with: most companies investing heavily in AI haven't seen a financial return. Not yet. Not outside the tech sector, anyway. And you know what? That's actually a useful thing to understand.

There's something clarifying about that statement. It cuts through the noise and gives us permission to think more carefully about what's really happening in the organizations we support and lead. If the money people aren't seeing the payoff yet, then we're not in a moment where we need to panic about missing the boat. We're in a moment where we get to think about what success actually looks like.

For those of us working in digital workplace functions, this lands differently than it might for someone in a boardroom. We're the ones building the systems where AI actually shows up in people's days. We're thinking about how a new tool gets adopted, how it fits into existing workflows, how it changes (or doesn't change) the way work actually happens. We're thinking about the person on the other end of a process, not just the line item on a budget.

The gap between investment and payoff doesn't feel like a failure when you frame it this way. It feels like real work. It feels like we're in the phase where implementation actually matters. Where thoughtful deployment beats rushing to show ROI. Where understanding your own organization—how it really works, what people actually need—becomes the competitive advantage.

There's something genuinely good about that position. It means the companies figuring things out right now aren't those with the biggest budgets. They're the ones paying attention to what happens on the ground. The ones where the digital workplace team has a seat at the table. The ones where adoption and change management get taken seriously from day one.

This also gives us all a moment to breathe. If the financial payoff isn't immediate, then the pressure to implement just for the sake of implementation should ease up a bit. We can focus on whether something actually makes sense for how our people work. We can pilot thoughtfully. We can learn what works and what doesn't without the anxiety that we're burning shareholder value.

The economist's observation isn't a doom statement. It's an invitation to be more deliberate. To think about what good actually looks like in your environment. To remember that technology adoption is a human process first, and a financial calculation second. That's not a setback. That's exactly where the real work begins.
