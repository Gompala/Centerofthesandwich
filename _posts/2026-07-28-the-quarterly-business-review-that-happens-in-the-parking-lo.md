There's this moment that happens in a lot of vendor relationships, usually around the third or fourth quarterly business review. Someone from the client side will catch you after the formal meeting ends, maybe in the hallway or literally in the parking lot, and say something like, "Look, the deck was fine, but here's what's actually frustrating us." And suddenly you're having the conversation that should have happened in the room but didn't, because the room wasn't safe for it.

The official QBR exists in this weird zone where everyone's performing their role. The vendor presents metrics that prove the relationship is working. The client nods and asks questions they already know the answers to. There's a slide about roadmap alignment. Someone mentions "value realization." Everyone leaves feeling like the meeting happened, which isn't quite the same as the meeting being honest.

The real QBR, the one that matters, happens off the record. It's usually someone saying "we're not actually using the feature you built last quarter and I'm not sure how to tell you without making it awkward." Or "your support team is great but we need someone who understands our specific workflow, not just your general process." Or the thing that's actually on people's minds: "Is this still working for both of us, or are we just continuing because stopping seems worse?"

What's interesting about this pattern is what it reveals about the relationship itself. If the real conversation can't happen in the scheduled meeting, that's information. It means there's enough distance, enough fear of awkwardness or conflict, that people are protecting the surface instead of protecting the partnership. You get politeness instead of honesty. You get a relationship that's technically fine but not actually good.

Some of this is just human nature. It's easier to be agreeable in front of a group. There's something about a formal setting that makes people cautious. But it's also revealing that we've normalized this split between the official conversation and the real one. The fact that "the real QBR happens after" is almost an expected thing in some relationships suggests something about how much actual trust exists.

The client who pulls you aside afterward isn't being disloyal or difficult. They're usually trying to save the relationship by being honest about it. They're saying "I care enough about this to tell you something harder than what I said in the meeting." The vendor who listens without getting defensive, who doesn't treat it as a complaint but as information, is usually the one who keeps the relationship.

But the better situation is when the actual room becomes safe enough that the real conversation happens there. Not the polished version, not the version that looks good on a slide. The "here's where we're struggling" and "here's where I think we could do better" version. That only happens if both sides stop performing and start being a little bit vulnerable about the state of things.

It's not that every relationship needs to be deeply personal or that formal meetings can't be valuable. It's just that the relationships that actually work are the ones where the hard conversation doesn't have to happen in the parking lot. Where the meeting room is honest enough for honesty.
