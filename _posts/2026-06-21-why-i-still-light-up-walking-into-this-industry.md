## The Moment Someone Gets It

There's a specific instant that still catches me off guard, even now. It happens when someone who's been frustrated with a tool or a process suddenly understands not just what it does, but why it matters to their actual work. Not because I explained it brilliantly. Usually because they finally had fifteen uninterrupted minutes to actually play with the thing.

I was watching this happen recently with a team getting comfortable with a new way of doing something. The breakthrough wasn't dramatic. No applause. Just someone saying, "Oh, I can actually do this faster now," and meaning it. That ripple effect, where one person gets it and then walks over to a colleague and shows them? That's the thread that's kept me here.

It sounds simple because it is simple. But it's also become rarer. There's so much noise about adoption rates and rollout metrics and getting everyone through the training module. The actual human moment of "this thing makes my work better" gets buried under process. When it happens anyway, it reminds me what the whole thing is actually for.

## The Conversations That Surprise You

Another one: sitting down with someone who thinks their job is broken because the technology is broken, and realizing halfway through the conversation that the technology isn't the problem at all. It's that nobody told them why a process changed. Or they're doing something in three steps that could be one step, but nobody showed them the one-step version exists.

These conversations are remarkable because they're not about being right or wrong. They're about discovery. You're both figuring something out together. And what gets me is how often the person on the other side of that conversation says something like, "I've been doing this the hard way for months and nobody asked me how it was going." That's not a technology failure. That's an attention failure.

But here's what makes me still want to be in this industry: these conversations are fixable. We can fix them right now. We just have to actually have them.

## The Unexpected Kindness

I keep bumping into people in this industry who care about things that don't go on a spreadsheet. Someone staying late to help a newer person understand a system. A leader fighting for proper training time instead of just accepting the crunch. Someone noticing that a colleague has gone quiet and checking in.

There's this current that runs through IT of people who could easily just maintain the minimum standard, do their job, move on. Instead, they're choosing to care about whether the thing actually works for the humans using it. They're choosing to explain things clearly. To listen when someone says a process isn't working.

That thread of actual human consideration in a field that's often framed as technical and cold? I notice it everywhere. It makes me feel less alone in thinking that this stuff matters because people matter.

## The Problems That Are Actually Solvable

Here's what's interesting about this moment in our industry: we're not struggling with unsolvable problems anymore. The core technology usually works. The infrastructure mostly holds up. What we're working on now is the human part. How do we make something intuitive? How do we help someone see why this change is worth their effort? How do we build something that respects how people actually work, not how an org chart says they should work?

That's a different kind of challenge than it was decades ago. It's less about "can we do this" and more about "should we do this this way." It requires curiosity about actual human experience. It requires humility. And it turns out there are a lot of people in this industry who have both.

Walking into that kind of work, where the problems are solvable and people genuinely care about solving them the right way, reminds me why I didn't burn out. Why I didn't move into something safer or quieter or more obviously prestigious.

It's because the moments still happen. Someone understands something. A conversation leads somewhere unexpected. A team figures out how to work better. And nobody had to force it. Nobody had to mandate it. It just rippled outward naturally because it actually made work better.

That's enough. It's always been enough.
