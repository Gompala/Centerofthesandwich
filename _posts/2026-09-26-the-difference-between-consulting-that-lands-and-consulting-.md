Good IT consulting isn't about bringing answers. It's about understanding what the organization is actually trying to become.

There's a real difference in how this work shows up. When consulting works well, it feels almost invisible at first. A consultant arrives and spends genuine time learning how things actually move through your organization. They sit in the real meetings, not the ones cleaned up for visitors. They ask about what matters to the people doing the work every day. They notice the small frictions that add up, and they also notice what's working beautifully and shouldn't be touched. They come back with recommendations that feel obvious once you hear them, the kind that make you think 'oh, of course, that's what we needed.'

When consulting doesn't land quite right, it often comes from a different starting place. The consultant arrives with a framework that's worked elsewhere, or a vision of what good looks like based on industry patterns. The work is usually thorough and well-intentioned, but it doesn't quite match the shape of your world. There's a gap between the recommendation and the reality of how your teams actually operate, what your constraints actually are, what your people actually care about.

The key difference seems to come down to curiosity versus knowledge. Consulting that works treats every organization as genuinely unique, not because every organization wants to feel special, but because it's true. Your digital workplace isn't generic. Your constraints aren't standard. Your culture isn't a template. A consultant who recognizes this from the beginning is already doing something valuable.

This matters for digital workplace leaders because you're the ones who have to live with the recommendations after the consultant leaves. You have to build the team around it, communicate it, implement it, defend it when things get hard. You need consulting that understands your actual situation, that respects the work you've already done, that sees clearly where you're trying to go.

The warmth of good consulting comes from this alignment. A consultant who gets your world, who sees your challenges clearly, who builds recommendations on what's true about your organization rather than what's true about organizations in general, that person becomes a real partner. They leave you with thinking that belongs to you, not thinking you have to defend.

If you're thinking about bringing in consulting support, or if you're working with consultants right now, it's worth paying attention to this. Does it feel like they're getting to know you, or like they're applying a pattern? Are the recommendations making you think more clearly about your own situation, or are they making you think about how to explain your situation to them? There's real value in that difference, and it's worth protecting.
