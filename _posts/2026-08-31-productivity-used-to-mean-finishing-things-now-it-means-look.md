A few years back, if you asked someone what productivity meant, you'd get a pretty straightforward answer: output. Finished work. Things shipped, delivered, completed. You were productive if you made progress on something that mattered. Simple.

Then something shifted. Not all at once, and not everywhere equally, but quietly enough that it's become the default so fast most people don't notice they're working under completely different rules.

Now productivity seems to mean something closer to "visibility of effort." It's about demonstrating that you're busy, that you're engaged, that you're responding and present. It's about your calendar looking full. It's about your status in Teams showing green. It's about being in meetings, sending messages, keeping up with threads. The actual output part got sort of quietly sidelined.

You can see this in how organizations measure and reward work now. They've become obsessed with activity metrics. Meeting attendance. Response time to messages. How quickly you come back online after being marked as away. Calendar utilization. Time spent in collaborative tools. Engagement in chat channels. Participation in standups. These are all things that are incredibly easy to track and quantify, which is probably why they're so appealing to manage by. But they have almost nothing to do with whether anything actually got built or created or solved.

The gap between these two definitions is where a lot of modern workplace frustration lives, though most people don't articulate it that way. You can be "productive" by every metric that's being measured and still feel like you haven't actually accomplished anything. You can have a calendar packed with meetings, rapid-fire Teams messages in every channel, immediate responses to every notification, perfect status availability, and still end up at the end of the week wondering when you actually did any real work.

What's interesting is that this probably didn't happen because anyone sat in a room and decided this was the new definition. It's more like a natural consequence of how remote and hybrid work operates. When you can't see someone, you default to measuring what you can see. Presence becomes a proxy for productivity. Participation becomes proof of engagement. The digital footprints people leave become the only real evidence you have, so they naturally become what you optimize for.

But here's where it gets weird: organizations keep hiring and promoting people who are genuinely good at output, the traditional kind, and then immediately putting them in systems that reward visibility instead. Then everyone gets frustrated when those two things don't align. You end up with people who are brilliant at producing actual work but struggle with being "productive" in the new sense because they don't light up every channel or sit in every meeting.

The uncomfortable part is that the new definition is way more exhausting to maintain. Looking busy all the time takes energy. Real work, deep work, work that requires thinking instead of just responding, is quieter. It doesn't generate activity metrics. It doesn't fill your calendar or spawn message threads. So there's actually an incentive structure now that works against actually getting things done well, which is a pretty weird place to have landed.

Most teams and leaders probably sense this is backward but don't know quite what to do about it. Going back to measuring output is harder than it sounds once you've got this much surveillance infrastructure in place. The visibility metrics are too convenient, too easy to pull from any platform, too defensible in a status report.

The real question isn't whether we should go back to measuring actual work instead of activity. It's whether organizations are going to figure out how to do both, or whether we're just going to keep shuffling people into systems that reward looking productive instead of being productive and call it progress.
