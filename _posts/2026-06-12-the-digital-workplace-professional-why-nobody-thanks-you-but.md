## The Job Nobody Wanted Until They Realized They Needed It

There's a peculiar moment that happens in almost every organization I've worked with over the years. A digital workplace professional does their job well, and absolutely nobody notices. Then they're sick for a week, and suddenly the CEO is asking if Slack is broken because their calendar invites aren't syncing properly.

This is the unsung hero problem in modern business, and it's been driving me up the wall because it doesn't have to be this way.

Digital workplace professionals are the people making sure that when your CEO presses send on an email at 11 PM, it actually gets sent. They're the ones troubleshooting why your video conference froze right before the board presentation. They're architecting the infrastructure that lets your company of 5,000 people actually talk to each other without everyone screaming into the void. And yet they spend most of their day explaining to executives why you can't just "move everything to the cloud" and call it a day.

## What Actually Counts as Work

Here's the thing about digital workplace roles: the visible work is maybe 20 percent of what they do. Anyone can reset a password or update a software license. What takes real skill is the invisible 80 percent. That's the strategy work. That's understanding that your company is about to undergo a massive reorganization, so the digital workplace team needs to quietly rebuild your directory structure before anyone even knows it's coming. That's anticipating that your sales team will add 200 new people next quarter and ensuring your email and collaboration infrastructure doesn't just buckle under the load.

It's also the thankless work of change management. Digital workplace professionals aren't just deploying technology. They're handling the human side of adoption. They're dealing with the department head who refuses to move from Outlook to Teams because they've used Outlook since 2003. They're creating training content that actually helps people instead of just pointing them at a 47-minute video. They're making sure your onboarding experience doesn't feel like you're learning to operate a submarine.

## Why They're Actually Strategic

Most organizations treat digital workplace as a cost center. They budget for licenses, maybe a helpdesk, and call it a day. This is backward thinking that costs companies millions in lost productivity.

A good digital workplace strategy determines whether your company can actually execute its business strategy. Full stop. If you're trying to build a collaborative, agile organization and your digital tools force people to work in silos, you've already lost. If you're trying to attract top talent and your onboarding experience is a nightmare, you're hiring people just to watch them leave.

The digital workplace professionals who understand this operate at a different level. They're not thinking about rolling out the latest version of something. They're thinking about what your business is trying to accomplish and how technology enables that. They're asking hard questions about whether you actually need seven different collaboration platforms, or whether that's just what happens when nobody has authority to say no.

## The Recognition Problem

I've watched brilliant digital workplace leaders get overlooked for promotions because their work is invisible. Their peers in application development get celebrated for shipping features that users see. Marketing gets celebrated for campaigns that drive revenue. But the person who reduced your password reset time from three days to 15 minutes? They get a pat on the back and another ticket queue.

This matters because it affects hiring and retention. The talented people who could be shaping how your organization works are instead getting recruited by companies that actually value what they do. You end up with a digital workplace function staffed by people who wanted IT work and that's what they got, rather than people who chose this role because they're passionate about making organizations work better.

## What Needs to Change

CTOs and IT leaders need to fight for this. Make digital workplace part of your strategy discussions, not an afterthought. When your CFO asks why you need a bigger budget for collaboration infrastructure, you should have a story about how that infrastructure enabled your company to go fully hybrid without losing productivity. When your CEO is talking about culture, your digital workplace strategy should be right there in the conversation.

And if you're a digital workplace professional reading this, stop accepting the sideline role. You have data on how people actually work. You see the patterns that executives miss. You understand friction points in your organization that nobody else can see. Bring that to the table. Frame your work in business terms, not technology terms. Show what happens when your systems go down. Show what happens when they work perfectly.

The best businesses in five years won't be the ones with the fanciest technology. They'll be the ones where technology actually works seamlessly in the background, letting people do their jobs without fighting their tools. That happens because somebody made it happen. That somebody deserves a seat at the table.
