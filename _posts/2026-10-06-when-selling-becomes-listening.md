The best digital workplace teams have quietly moved from pitching solutions to understanding what people actually need.

There's something genuinely refreshing happening in how organizations approach their digital services these days. The conversation has shifted. Instead of starting with what you have to offer, the conversation now begins with what people are actually trying to do.

This isn't a small thing. It changes everything about how a team shows up. When you walk in thinking 'here's what we built,' you're in one kind of conversation. When you walk in thinking 'what's getting in your way,' you're in a completely different one. The person you're talking to feels it. They relax a little. They know you're genuinely curious about their situation, not just waiting for your turn to talk.

What makes this shift interesting is that it's not really about abandoning solutions at all. It's about recognizing that a solution only matters if it actually solves something real. A digital workplace platform, a service catalog, an automation workflow, a communication tool, a training resource—these things are only valuable in context. And context only becomes clear when you actually understand the problem first.

The teams doing this well tend to have a particular kind of patience. They ask questions. They listen to answers. They push back gently when they need to, not to win an argument but to make sure they're understanding correctly. They spend time in the spaces where work actually happens instead of just talking to stakeholders in boardrooms. They watch what people do when they think no one's paying attention. That's where the real insight is.

What's lovely about this approach is how it affects the relationship between the digital workplace team and the people they serve. When someone feels genuinely understood, when they can see that you're trying to solve their actual problem rather than install something you believe in, the whole dynamic changes. Trust shows up. Collaboration becomes easier. People are more willing to work with you on solutions because they know you're actually invested in whether those solutions work.

It also tends to produce better outcomes. When you start with the problem instead of the solution, you often end up with something more elegant, more useful, and more likely to actually get adopted. Sometimes the answer isn't even a new tool. Sometimes it's a process change, or a conversation that needed to happen, or something that already exists being used differently.

The thing that makes this approach feel genuinely good is that it respects the intelligence and experience of the people you're serving. It says: I don't have all the answers, but I'm genuinely interested in understanding your world. Let's figure this out together. That's the kind of partnership that makes digital workplace work actually feel like work that matters.

It's a shift worth thinking about, whether you're just starting to move in this direction or you're already there.
