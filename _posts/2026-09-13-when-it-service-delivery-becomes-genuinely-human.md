Empathy isn't soft. It's the connective tissue that turns IT operations into something that actually works for people.

Think about the last time someone helped you solve a problem and actually understood what you needed, not just what you asked for. They listened to the thing beneath the thing. They thought about your context, your constraints, your day. That moment probably shaped how you felt about the whole interaction, regardless of whether the solution was technically flawless.

This is what's shifting in IT service delivery, and it's genuinely worth noticing. Organizations are recognizing that the quality of a service isn't just measured in uptime or resolution time. It's measured in whether the person on the other end feels like their situation was understood.

What makes this shift interesting is that it's not coming from somewhere abstract. It's emerging because the work itself demands it. When you're supporting people navigating hybrid work, managing multiple tools, dealing with the friction between systems that don't quite talk to each other, you can't do that well without understanding what their actual day looks like. You need to know whether someone is troubleshooting at their kitchen table while managing two video calls, or whether they have space to focus. You need to understand that 'it's not working' might mean something different to an executive than it does to an engineer, not because anyone's being difficult, but because the stakes and the workflows are genuinely different.

The teams doing this well are building it into how they actually operate. They're training people to ask better questions. They're designing support workflows that create space for understanding rather than just rushing to the next ticket. They're using data about support interactions not just to measure efficiency, but to understand patterns in what people actually struggle with. This is empathy as a practice, not a feeling.

What's particularly good about this moment is that organizations aren't framing this as a soft skill add-on. They're treating it as foundational to service excellence. Because it is. Someone who deeply understands what you're trying to accomplish can help you faster, suggest better solutions, and prevent problems before they become crises. Empathy and effectiveness aren't in tension. They're partners.

The teams supporting digital workplaces right now are in an interesting position. You're not just keeping systems running. You're helping people work in ways that are still relatively new to all of us. That requires genuine attention to what the experience actually feels like from where they're sitting. That's empathy at work, and it's becoming clearer every day that it's not a luxury. It's core to getting the job done well.
