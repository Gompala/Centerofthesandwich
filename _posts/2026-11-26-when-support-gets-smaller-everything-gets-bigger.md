The math looks clean on a spreadsheet: reduce headcount in support operations, improve the bottom line, everyone wins. Except the people trying to actually use the thing.

There's something worth sitting with here, because it's not about villains or mismanagement. It's about how the pressures that shape business decisions can quietly reshape what users experience, and how those two things are far more connected than a P&L statement suggests.

When a vendor reduces support staff to increase profitability, they're essentially making a bet that the product will work so well and so intuitively that fewer people will need help. And sometimes that's exactly right. But digital workplaces are complex. They integrate with other systems. They need to adapt to how your organization actually works, not how anyone imagined you'd work. People get stuck. Questions come up at three in the afternoon. Something that worked yesterday stops working today.

Support teams do more than answer tickets. They're watching patterns. They see which features confuse people, which integrations break under real-world conditions, what actually matters to your organization. That feedback loop, that human layer of observation and care, is how products improve. It's how they stay responsive to what users really need.

When support gets lean, something shifts. Response times stretch. The wisdom that comes from thousands of conversations with real users thins out. Issues that could have been caught and fixed become frustrations that compound. The person trying to get their work done doesn't see a business optimization. They see a service that feels like it's working against them instead of for them.

This creates a strange kind of distance between the vendor and the people using their product. The organization that built something to solve a problem ends up less aware of whether it's actually solving that problem for anyone. And the people who depend on that product find themselves problem-solving on their own, talking to peers instead of experts, burning time and goodwill on something that was supposed to save both.

There's real value in thinking this through. If you're evaluating a vendor or renewing a contract, support quality is worth taking seriously. It's part of what you're actually buying. And if you work in digital workplace leadership, it's worth keeping in mind as you think about your own support operations. The constraint that feels like efficiency often costs far more than it saves, just not in a way that shows up clearly on anyone's spreadsheet.

Good support isn't a cost center. It's the connection between what was built and what actually works.
