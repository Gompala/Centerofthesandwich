There's something quietly powerful about what happens when the people making decisions are also the people using the tools every day.

When an executive sits down with the same collaboration platform, the same communication channels, the same workflows that everyone else relies on, something shifts. Not dramatically. Not with fanfare. But genuinely.

They encounter the small friction points that most people just learn to work around. That notification that comes through at the wrong moment. The search that doesn't quite find what you're looking for. The way information flows in some directions smoothly and gets stuck in others. They experience these moments not as abstract complaints in a feedback survey, but as actual interruptions to their own day.

More importantly, they see what actually works. They notice when a colleague's response time improves because they finally understood how to use a feature properly. They feel the relief of finding information quickly. They experience the difference between a tool that feels like it was built for humans and one that feels like it was built for compliance.

This kind of proximity changes perspective in ways that reports and user research simply cannot. A leader living in the same digital environment develops intuition. They start thinking about these tools the way the people using them eight hours a day think about them. Not as solutions implemented, but as living systems that shape how work actually happens.

The organizations that seem to get this right share something in common: their leaders are genuinely curious about the experience their teams have. They're not auditing or investigating. They're just working. They're asking real questions about what they see. They're noticing patterns. They're understanding that the digital workplace isn't a separate thing that IT maintains—it's where the actual work of the organization lives.

This also tends to create a different kind of conversation between leadership and the people managing these environments. When an executive has felt the frustration of a broken process, they understand why solving it matters. They get why the team is excited about a small improvement that might seem invisible to outsiders. They're operating from shared reality rather than secondhand interpretation.

It's not complicated. It's just alignment. It's people understanding that the tools that enable work are worth understanding deeply, and that the best way to understand them is to use them. The warmth and genuineness that comes from that shared experience—from leaders and individual contributors navigating the same digital landscape together—that's where better decision-making happens. That's where organizations start moving together in the same direction.
