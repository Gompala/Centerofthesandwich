## The Service Desk Was Always the Worst Job in IT

Let me tell you what a service desk shift felt like in 2003. You'd sit down at your desk, coffee in hand, ready to actually solve something interesting. Then the ticket queue would load and you'd see 47 variations of "my password doesn't work." Not because people were stupid. Just because that was the job. Ninety percent of your day was tier-one triage. Someone asking why their email was slow. Someone else whose printer driver got confused again. Someone's Caps Lock was on and they didn't know it.

The talented people? They'd stick it out for eight months, learn the system architecture, and immediately move to infrastructure or engineering. The ones who stayed were either genuinely patient saints or had nowhere else to go. And if you were a manager, you cycled through bodies like a restaurant cycles through kitchen staff.

Nobody planned to have a 30-year career in service desk support. They planned to escape it.

## The Thing That's Actually Changing Now

What's shifted isn't magic. It's not that AI solved the hard problems — the empathy part, the complex troubleshooting, the situations where you need actual judgment. Those still need humans. What's happened is that AI finally made the repetitive stuff go away, and that changes everything.

You know what happens when you remove the soul-crushing layer from a job? The job becomes something a person might actually want to do.

I watched this play out at a financial services company last year. They implemented an AI ticketing system that handled the obvious stuff — password resets, basic connectivity, "how do I access this application" questions. Not perfectly. But good enough that it handled about 40 percent of the volume before a human ever touched it. The service desk team went from "batch process 200 tickets" to "actually solve 80 tickets."

Want to know what happened? People stopped leaving. And the ones who stayed started acting like they had actual careers.

## What Good Actually Looks Like

The people who work at that financial company's service desk now spend their time on actual problems. A manager can't connect to the VPN and the automated troubleshooting didn't work? That's interesting. A department's email is routing to the wrong server? That requires actual thinking. Someone has a specific workflow problem that touches three different systems? Now you're solving something.

They also started getting bored less often, which means they started caring about getting better at their jobs. One of the technicians taught herself ServiceNow customization. Another started learning Python to write small automation scripts. A third became genuinely interested in security because she was seeing real access control issues instead of just resetting credentials.

The manager told me the turnover dropped from 45 percent annually to about 12 percent. The team actually trained each other now instead of just training whoever came through the door. And here's the thing nobody mentions when they talk about AI in the service desk — the quality of solutions got better. Not because the AI was smarter. Because humans who weren't exhausted made better decisions.

## The Messy Reality

None of this happened automatically, by the way. The company had to actually think about what the service desk team's job was after the AI handled the noise. They didn't just drop an AI tool on the desk and walk away. They restructured the workflow. They trained people on new tools. They let the team help design which problems the AI should handle and which ones should go straight to humans.

And yes, the AI made mistakes. It still does. But they built a process where mistakes got caught and corrected instead of ignored.

What's interesting is that this model works for other industries too. A healthcare organization uses similar AI triage for their IT support requests. A manufacturing company uses it for facilities tickets. The pattern is the same everywhere — remove the repetitive triage layer, and suddenly the work becomes something humans find meaningful.

## Why This Matters More Than You Think

Service desk work was never really a bad job. It was a good job buried under an avalanche of repetitive tasks that made it feel like a dead end. The people were fine. The work environment was fine. The computers worked fine.

It was the volume of sameness that broke people.

AI doesn't make service desk work glamorous. It's still support work. It's still helping people figure out why their computer is doing something weird. But now it's the interesting part of that work without the thousand death-by-a-thousand-cuts repetitions first.

You can actually build a career in this space now. Which means the people staying longer are the ones who want to be there, not the ones who haven't escaped yet.

That's the real transformation. Not the technology. The fact that we finally made the work worth staying for.
