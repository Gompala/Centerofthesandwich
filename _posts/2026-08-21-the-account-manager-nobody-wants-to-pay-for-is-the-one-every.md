There's a moment that happens about six months into most digital workplace implementations where someone in procurement asks why they're still paying for account management. The software is deployed. The users have access. Why does the vendor still need to have someone assigned to the account?

It's a reasonable question if you're looking at a spreadsheet. It's a catastrophic one if you're actually trying to run something.

The gap between what organizations think they're buying and what they actually need tends to be measured in relationships. Not the forced networking kind. The kind where someone on the vendor side knows that your finance team uses Teams for approvals in a specific way that nobody documented, or that your leadership group has strong opinions about how search results surface, or that your real problem isn't the software at all but the fact that two departments are approaching the same tool in completely different ways and creating chaos.

A good account manager isn't a salesperson pretending to care. They're someone who sits with you long enough to understand how your organization actually works, not how the org chart says it works. They notice that the teams using the platform most effectively have different needs than the ones struggling. They catch the thing your project team was supposed to handle but nobody remembered because priorities shifted. They know who the real influencers are in your organization, not who the titles say they should be.

And then they act on it. They'll push back when you're about to make a decision that the vendor knows tends to go badly. They'll adjust how support gets delivered because they understand your team's rhythm better than the generic support queue does. They'll flag the thing that's about to break before it breaks, not after.

But here's why these relationships keep getting cut: they're invisible work. You can't point to a feature delivery and say that account management did that. You can't measure the headache that didn't happen because someone was paying attention. The wins look like absence of problems, and absence is cheaper to cut than presence.

When an organization decides to move to a lower-touch or fully self-service vendor model, the pitch is always about efficiency and cost. Sometimes that's exactly right. But sometimes what they're actually doing is shifting from a model where someone external was helping the organization think clearly to one where the organization just manages alone. The internal team gets busier. The problems that a vendor account manager would have caught now become internal escalations. The technology works fine. The organization just gets slightly worse at using it.

There's also the question of who benefits from the relationship. The account manager benefits because they have ongoing work. The vendor benefits because retention gets better. But the organization benefits most, and that's the part that's hard to justify in a budget meeting. You're essentially paying for external perspective on something you're convinced you can figure out internally. Except you probably can't, not at the depth that actually matters.

The vendors who are winning right now aren't the ones with the most features or the lowest prices. They're the ones who've structured their delivery model so the relationship is baked in, not bolted on. Where the account manager isn't a cost center but part of how the service actually works. Where staying in touch with clients isn't something that happens when there's a problem to solve.

Because the truth is simpler than budgets make it sound: organizations that feel like they have a real partner in their digital workplace vendor are happier and more successful with that vendor. Not because the features are better. Because someone is actually thinking about them.
