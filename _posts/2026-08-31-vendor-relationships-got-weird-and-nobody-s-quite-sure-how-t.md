There's a specific moment in a vendor relationship where things shift. It usually happens quietly, in a Slack message that takes three days to answer, or a renewal conversation that starts with "we need to talk about pricing." A lot of organizations are living in that moment right now, and it doesn't feel accidental.

The framing everyone uses is disruption. Supply chain issues, economic uncertainty, the whole thing. Fair enough. But what actually changed was simpler and weirder: vendors stopped pretending to care about specific customers.

For years, the deal was pretty straightforward. You'd sign a contract, get assigned an account manager who actually knew your environment, and have someone to call when things went sideways. It wasn't always smooth, but there was at least a person whose job was partly to keep you happy. That person had skin in the game because retaining you mattered to their numbers.

That model is barely recognizable now. Account managers got reorganized into tier structures based on contract value. Support got segmented by license level. Every conversation started requiring escalation. And somewhere in there, the incentive structure flipped. Instead of an account manager trying to keep you happy to protect a renewal, you got a sales team trying to upsell you, separated from any consequence if you left.

It feels like vendors decided that winning new customers was cheaper than keeping existing ones. Which, mathematically, might actually be true for them. But it creates this strange dynamic where the vendor relationship becomes transactional in a way it never quite was before. You're not a partner anymore. You're just a contract number with a renewal date.

The kicker is that organizations are actually okay with this, just for different reasons than vendors intended. A lot of places realized they don't need the friendship. They need clarity, consistency, and someone who'll actually fix problems. If a vendor can't deliver on those basics, it doesn't matter how many relationship-building coffees they used to buy.

So what replaced the old model isn't necessarily worse. It's just different. Organizations are building their own vendor management practices instead of relying on vendors to manage the relationship. They're treating contracts like actual contracts, not gentleman's agreements. They're diversifying vendors specifically to avoid the single-point-of-dependency problem that old relationships created. And they're keeping vendor interactions functional rather than friendly.

The weird part is watching vendors realize this happened to them without quite understanding why. There's still this assumption in the enterprise software world that customers will stick around because switching is hard or because they're locked into a contract. Which, sure, technically true. But it misses that customers are no longer operating under the assumption that vendors are looking out for their interests. That changes everything about how you approach the relationship.

Vendor relationships didn't get worse in the last couple of years. They just became honest. And that's probably better for everyone, even if it feels less comfortable. There's something cleaner about admitting that this is a business transaction, not a partnership. Once you do, you can actually get to the work that matters.
