Employees aren't asking for less structure or more freedom. They're asking for something quieter: to feel genuinely seen.

There's a particular kind of visibility that comes with working in digital workplace functions. Your work touches almost everything. You're the person who knows why the network runs the way it does, or how the helpdesk actually handles three hundred requests a day, or what employees really need from their tools to do their work well. You see patterns that most people don't see because you're paying attention to how things actually move through an organization.

What employees wish leadership understood is that this visibility works both ways. They want to be seen too, not as abstract groups or personas, but as people who are trying to do good work within real constraints. They want leadership to understand that when someone complains about a process, they're usually not complaining about the process itself. They're telling you something real about what it costs them to work around it.

They're not asking for perfection. Digital workplace professionals understand better than almost anyone that perfection isn't the goal. The goal is workability. It's creating spaces where people can do what they're hired to do without unnecessary friction, where tools actually serve the work rather than getting in the way, where someone can find what they need without wondering if they're looking in the right place.

There's also something about being heard on timing and reality. Employees understand that big changes take time. They understand that you can't fix everything at once. What they want to be seen as is people who know their own situation. Someone working in the field knows things that no amount of data alone can show. That knowledge matters, and when it's taken seriously, something different happens in how people feel about their work.

Leadership that genuinely understands this creates something interesting. It's not looser or more permissive. It's actually clearer. When people feel seen, when they understand that their actual daily reality matters to the decisions being made, they become better partners in building something that works. They're more invested. They think more carefully about what they're asking for. They become part of solving the puzzle instead of just living with the results.

The good news is that this understanding is completely achievable. It doesn't require new tools or new meetings. It requires the same thing that makes digital workplace work in the first place: genuine attention to how things actually function, and real respect for the people doing the work.

That's worth thinking about, and worth building toward.
