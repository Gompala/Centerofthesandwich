There's something interesting happening in IT departments right now that nobody's quite naming directly. People are leaving. Not in a dramatic, rage-quit way. More like a slow fade. They're taking roles that pay the same or less but somehow feel lighter. They're moving to smaller companies. They're leaving tech entirely. And when you actually talk to them about it, the word that keeps coming up isn't "salary" or "growth" or any of the things HR typically optimizes for.

It's exhaustion.

The burnout conversation in IT isn't new, but something has shifted in how organizations are responding to it. For years, burnout was treated like a personal problem. Work-life balance was your responsibility. Mental health was your weekend. If you were struggling, that meant you needed better time management, or maybe you weren't cut out for the role.

Now, increasingly, IT leadership is recognizing that this framing doesn't work anymore. Not because burnout suddenly became fashionable to discuss, but because the operational math doesn't add up. When your senior engineers are hitting the wall, you lose institutional knowledge faster than you can document it. When good people leave, you spend months replacing them. When teams are perpetually understaffed, the remaining people get stretched thinner, which creates the exact conditions that made the first people leave.

So what are organizations actually doing about it?

The honest answer is: it's mixed. Some teams have discovered that on-call rotations don't have to be three weeks on, one week off. Some are actively hiring to reduce dependency on specific people. Some are saying no to projects that would require "just one more round" of crunch. These sound obvious when written down, but they're genuinely happening at companies where they weren't before.

Other organizations are still mostly talking about the problem. They're doing well-being initiatives. Free meditation apps. Team outings. Quiet quitting awareness sessions. The stuff that makes the org chart feel like it's addressing something without actually changing what makes people exhausted in the first place.

The gap between these approaches is revealing. In one case, the organization is restructuring work. In the other, they're asking people to be better at handling the same work.

What's becoming clearer is that IT burnout isn't really about hours worked, though hours matter. It's about whether people feel heard when they say something isn't sustainable. It's about whether their manager has actual authority to push back on demands instead of just sympathizing while passing them down. It's about whether "we know we're asking too much" gets followed by a plan to ask less, or just becomes the new normal.

The companies that seem to be moving the needle aren't eliminating pressure. That's not possible in IT. They're changing how visible and negotiable the pressure is. Teams know what's coming. Priorities are transparent. If something new lands, something else actually stops. It's not magical. It's just that people can tolerate a lot when they understand it and believe it's temporary.

The interesting part is that this conversation is finally moving beyond the question of whether burnout is real and toward the actual operational question: what are we willing to change about how we work so people don't have to leave in order to survive here? Some organizations have a real answer. Others are still working on the phrasing.
