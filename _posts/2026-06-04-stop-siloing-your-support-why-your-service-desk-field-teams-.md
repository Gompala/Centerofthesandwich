## The Problem Nobody Wants to Admit

Your service desk takes a ticket. They don't have the answer, so they dump it to infrastructure. Infrastructure pokes at it for two days, then realizes it's actually an application issue. Application support finally looks at it and says "this is a field services problem." Meanwhile, your customer is three weeks into a resolution that should have taken three hours.

I've watched this cycle play out at companies with 50 people and companies with 5,000. The org chart says these teams are connected. Reality says they're operating on different planets.

The fundamental issue is that most IT organizations design their support structure around internal convenience, not customer outcomes. We create towers because towers are easy to manage. We measure success within towers because we control the metrics. And then we're shocked when customers hate us.

## Why Integration Fails (And Why You Keep Trying)

You've probably already bought a tool to solve this. Maybe it's ServiceNow, maybe it's Atlassian, maybe it's something else. The tool promised unified ticketing, integrated workflows, visibility across all layers. Installation went fine. Implementation is where things got weird.

Three months in, your service desk is still doing their own thing. Field services hasn't actually changed their process. Infrastructure is using the system for ticketing but still resolving issues the way they always did. Application support is... honestly, where is application support in all of this?

The tool didn't fail. Your approach to using it did.

Most organizations treat tool implementation as a technical problem. It isn't. It's a human problem wrapped in software. You can have the best platform in the world, but if your service desk supervisor doesn't trust infrastructure to actually solve problems, they'll route everything differently. If field services doesn't get credit for the work they do, they'll document things in a way that makes integration look terrible. If application support operates in total isolation, they'll become impossible to reach from anywhere else in the organization.

## What Actually Works

Start with honesty about how work actually moves through your organization right now. Don't map how you think it works. Don't map how it should work. Map how it really works. Talk to your people. A service desk technician who's been doing this for five years knows where every shortcut is, where every real problem lives. Listen to them.

Once you understand the real flow, design your integration around removing friction points, not around organizational elegance. If field services always has to call infrastructure to confirm parts availability before they quote a customer, that's a friction point. Build an integration that lets them see availability directly. If application support never knows when a customer has already contacted field services, that's a friction point. Fix it.

Now here's the part most organizations get wrong: make sure every tower benefits immediately from integration. Service desk gets better visibility into what's actually happening with their escalations. Infrastructure gets better context from the service desk about what customers are experiencing. Field services gets actual data about what's causing repeat problems instead of guessing. Application support gets to see patterns across the whole organization instead of just their own tickets. When everyone gets something valuable on day one, adoption stops being a management directive and starts being common sense.

## Managing the People Across the Towers

You need clear escalation paths, but not rigid ones. A good escalation process should be a guide, not a prison. Service desk should be able to pull infrastructure in early if they smell a platform issue. Infrastructure should be able to loop in application support without making it feel like a pass-the-buck moment. Field services should have a direct line to infrastructure when they're troubleshooting something that might be network-related.

Measure what you actually care about. Most organizations measure tickets per person and resolution time by tower. Those metrics actively discourage collaboration. Start measuring customer impact instead. Did we solve this customer's problem completely? Did we solve it faster because different towers worked together? Did we prevent the same issue from happening again? Those measurements change behavior.

Give your teams permission to own the customer experience end to end, even when that means stepping outside their tower. The service desk person who understands enough about infrastructure to ask smart questions is more valuable than someone who just says "I'll open a ticket." The field service tech who can read an application log and provide context to app support is more valuable than someone who just collects error codes. Invest in that cross-training. It costs less than you think.

## The Real Test

You'll know integration is actually working when a customer can describe a problem once and get a complete answer, regardless of which tower owns what. When a ticket moves between teams because of work flow, not because of handoff avoidance. When people from different towers actually want to work together because it makes their jobs easier, not because management told them to.

That's the outcome that matters. Everything else is implementation details.
