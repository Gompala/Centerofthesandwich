Enterprise video conferencing isn't a novelty anymore. It's simply how we work together now, and that's a genuinely interesting place to be.

What's worth paying attention to is how normalized this all has become. Video conferencing platforms have moved from 'something we use when we have to' to 'the default way we connect.' That shift happened quietly, but it's profound. When everyone on your team assumes a meeting will have both in-person and remote participants, when you can share a screen as naturally as pointing at something in a room, when the technology mostly just works without anyone thinking about it, you're looking at something that's reached a kind of maturity.

That maturity shows up in how organizations are thinking about these tools now. Digital workplace teams are spending less energy troubleshooting and more energy thinking about the human side: how do we make sure remote participants feel genuinely included? How do we preserve the informal moments that used to happen between meetings? How do we build connection when some people are colocated and others are spread across time zones? These are the questions that matter now, and they're better questions than 'will this technology work?'

There's also something nice happening around choice and comfort. Most organizations have settled into a rhythm where they've chosen their primary platform, but people aren't anxious about interoperability the way they used to be. There's an acceptance that you might use three or four different video platforms depending on context, and that's just fine. A tool for all-hands meetings might be different from your team's daily standup tool, which is different from your collaboration space. People move between them without it feeling like friction.

The role of video conferencing in remote and hybrid work has also given organizations permission to think differently about what an office even is. When video conferencing isn't a second-class citizen compared to in-person meetings, the entire conversation changes. Offices become places where you gather for specific purposes, not places where you have to be to participate. That's freed up a lot of creative thinking about workspace design and about who works where and when.

What's genuinely worth appreciating is how far we've come on the boring, essential stuff. Bandwidth isn't a constraint. Audio quality is reliable. Security is baked in. The platform you choose probably integrates smoothly with your calendar and your chat and your files. These things weren't always true, and the fact that they are now means that video conferencing has truly become infrastructure.

The question for digital workplace leaders now isn't really about video conferencing itself anymore. It's about how to build the culture and processes and spaces that make remote and hybrid work feel seamless and human. That's the next layer of thinking, and it's the part that actually makes the difference in how people experience their work. Video conferencing was the foundation. Now it's time to build something good on top of it.
