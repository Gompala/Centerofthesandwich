When measurement becomes the main thing, something quietly shifts in how people show up to work.

There's a particular moment that leaders in digital workplace functions notice—usually without fanfare, sometimes without even realizing it happened. It's when the conversation moves from 'how are our people experiencing their work' to 'what exactly are our people doing with their time.' The shift is subtle enough that it doesn't announce itself. No one decides to make it happen. It just does, the way seasons change.

The tools make it easy. We have wonderful technology that tells us when people logged in, how long they spent on tasks, which applications they used, when they were away from their desks. The data is there, clean and organized, waiting to be understood. And understanding is good. Visibility is useful. Knowing what's happening in your organization helps you make decisions.

But there's something worth thinking about in the difference between 'understanding what people do' and 'making sure people know they're being watched.' One feels like leadership. The other feels like something else entirely.

The beautiful thing about valuing staff—actually valuing them, not just saying it—is that it creates space for people to bring their best thinking to work. It creates permission to solve problems creatively, to take thoughtful risks, to spend twenty minutes thinking through something because it matters. When people feel valued, they're generous with their effort in ways that no amount of tracking can measure. They stay longer than they have to. They care about outcomes that don't benefit them directly. They help colleagues solve problems. They think about the work even when they're not being observed.

When the focus tips toward tracking, something different happens. Not something dramatic. Just a quieter shift. People work differently when they know they're being measured on activity rather than impact. They optimize for what's visible. They think about the optics of their time. They bring less of their whole selves.

Executive teams who find themselves drawn to deeper tracking often genuinely want to understand their organization better. That's a good instinct. The insight worth sitting with is whether understanding what people do will actually help you build the kind of workplace where people want to do their best work, or whether it just gives you better information about a less engaged team.

It's not about choosing between accountability and warmth. It's about remembering that the best measurement of whether digital workplace functions are working isn't the data you can collect. It's the culture you're creating. And culture lives in how people feel when they come to work, not in how many minutes they spent there.
