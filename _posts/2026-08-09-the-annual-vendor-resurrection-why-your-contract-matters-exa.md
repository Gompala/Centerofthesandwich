There's a specific moment every year when your vendor suddenly cares deeply about you. Not because anything changed in the product. Not because you've been sending daily feedback to their support team. It's because your contract is about to expire and the renewal date is circled on someone's dashboard in red.

It's predictable enough that you could set a calendar reminder. Three months before expiration, you'll get an email from someone new at the company with a title that sounds made up. Account Executive. Solutions Architect. Digital Transformation Strategist. The email will be personalized enough to prove they looked at your company's LinkedIn page, but not so much that it feels creepy. They'll mention the "value you've generated together" as though you've been texting all year.

Then come the check-ins. The "just wanted to see how things are going" message in Teams that would be charming if it weren't transparently about money. The invitation to a "strategic review call" where they want to understand your current challenges. And honestly, some of these conversations are fine. Sometimes they're actually useful. But there's a specific flavor to them that only exists during renewal season. It's the attention you'd get from a friend who only calls when they need to borrow something, except they're trying very hard to pretend that's not what's happening.

The weird part is that the vendor's sudden attentiveness is actually useful data. That call where they're asking about your workflows and pain points? That's real information gathering. The discount they're suddenly willing to offer? That reveals what they were probably always willing to do. The "new features we've built specifically for enterprise customers like you" that mysteriously weren't highlighted in your last 18 months of updates? Yeah, that too.

But here's what makes it genuinely strange: this entire cycle exists because the relationship was allowed to atrophy for the previous eleven months. There's no world in which a vendor should only check in when money is on the table. A healthy vendor relationship would include regular business reviews, proactive outreach about features that matter to your use case, and actual engagement about whether the product is still solving your problems. Instead, most vendors operate on a cycle that amounts to: months of radio silence, then a sudden panic when renewal approaches.

Some vendors are better about this than others. The ones that actually maintain ongoing relationships during the year don't feel frantic when renewal time comes around. Their check-ins don't land like awkward corporate speed dating. But they're the exception.

The real thing happening here isn't complicated. Vendors are optimized for acquisition and renewal, not for retention through actual service. The incentive structure just points that way. A product team that makes things better doesn't necessarily move renewal rates, but a sales team that remembers to follow up on the renewal date absolutely does. So that's where the resources flow.

If you're managing vendor relationships, you probably already know this. You've felt that tonal shift when the renewal date gets close. Maybe you've even used it strategically, knowing that suddenly the vendor will move on pricing or features right before they lose you. It's not exactly a con, but it's not a genuine partnership either. It's just the shape that these relationships have taken because nobody's optimizing for anything else.

The contract renewal season exists because we've all accepted that this is how it works. And it does work, technically. Vendors keep customers, customers keep software they're using, and both sides get through their fiscal year intact. But the fact that it requires this annual song and dance every single time is worth sitting with. It's telling us something about what these relationships actually are.
