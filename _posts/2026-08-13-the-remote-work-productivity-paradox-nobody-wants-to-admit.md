There's a person on your team who disappears into their home office and emerges eight hours later with more done than they'd accomplish in a week at the office. There's also probably someone else whose Slack status says "in focus time" while they're actually three tabs deep into existential panic about whether they've made a terrible mistake.

We've spent three years treating this like a problem with an answer. Either remote work is good for productivity or it isn't. Either your employees need to come back to the office or they don't. Managers debate it like it's a binary switch, when really it's more like discovering that some people are wired to need a crowd around them to function, and others need absolute silence, and there's nothing wrong with either situation.

The thing is, we already knew this about everything else. Some people write better in coffee shops. Some people need a completely empty room. Some people think best while walking. Some people need to sit still. But somehow when the office became optional, we acted surprised that humans didn't all respond the same way.

What's actually happening is less about "remote work" as a concept and more about the individual friction points that exist between how someone's brain works and their environment. For some people, the office was full of those friction points. The open plan meant constant interruption. The commute meant already depleted mental energy before the workday started. The fluorescent lights and the ambient noise and the feeling of being watched all combined into a system that was actively fighting against their productivity.

Remove those friction points and they don't just work better, they work *differently*. Better focus. Fewer context switches. The ability to actually think about hard problems instead of constantly reacting. They're not more disciplined at home. They're just less exhausted.

For other people, the office provided something crucial that home doesn't. The structure. The presence of other people solving similar problems. The accidental conversations that spark ideas. The ambient awareness of what everyone else is working on. For them, remote work isn't freedom, it's isolation with higher expectations.

This gets framed as a weakness in remote workers, like they "need the office" in a way that suggests dependency or lack of initiative. But it's really just about information flow and external structure. Some brains work better when information arrives passively, through proximity. Some brains work better when they have to be intentional about seeking it out.

The productivity question isn't really about location. It's about fit. And the uncomfortable thing for most organizations is that fit is individual, not universal, and it doesn't map neatly onto roles or departments or seniority levels.

The question that matters isn't "should people work from home?" It's "what does this person actually need to do their best work?" And then building a system flexible enough to accommodate the answer, even when the answer is "something different from everyone else." That's messier than a mandate either way. It requires actually paying attention to how people work instead of choosing a model and hoping everyone fits into it.

But that's the part that tends to get skipped. It's easier to decide everyone comes back on Tuesday, or everyone stays home, than to build something that actually matches human variation. So we keep debating remote work like it's a universal thing when it's really just a bunch of individuals in different circumstances.
