There's a specific moment in vendor relationships where things shift from transactional to actually functional. Your dedicated manager knows your setup. They know which person on your team gets anxious about API changes. They've learned that Tuesday mornings are bad for calls because of your standup. When something breaks at 2 PM on a Friday, you text them and they actually get back to you.

Then one day you get an email. Your manager is moving into a new role. Or they're being promoted. Or they're joining a different division. Sometimes there's no email at all, just a new name in the Slack channel with a cheerful "excited to work with you" message.

The replacement is fine. They're competent. They're probably nice. But they don't know anything. They don't know that you have a standing weekly sync. They don't know you've been asking for a feature request for six months. They certainly don't know that the previous manager promised you'd get bumped up on the priority list when engineering had bandwidth.

You start over.

What's interesting is how consistent this pattern is across vendors. It doesn't seem to matter if you're working with a scrappy startup or a massive enterprise platform. The moment the relationship becomes actually valuable, the person who understands it gets rotated out. It's like the vendor has a timer, and when it goes off, they shuffle the deck.

There are a few things happening here, probably all at once. One is that good managers get promoted. That's ostensibly fine, except the vendor hasn't built a system where a manager's departure doesn't crater the relationship. They treat the person as the relationship rather than a node in a functioning infrastructure. When that person leaves, everything they held in their head leaves with them.

Another is that vendors often use account management as a training ground. New managers get assigned to accounts, they learn the business, they get good at it, then they move somewhere else. From the vendor's perspective, this is efficient. From your perspective, you've just trained someone for their next job and gotten nothing out of it.

There's also the reality that satisfied customers are less urgent than at-risk ones. A manager with a strong relationship might get pulled toward an account that's churning or about to churn. The thinking is probably that experienced managers should be deployed where the friction is highest. Which makes sense strategically and is terrible for you.

The frustrating part is that this is solvable. Vendors could document account history so transitions don't require rebuilding from zero. They could slow down manager rotations on strategic accounts. They could make the relationship less dependent on one person knowing things and more dependent on systems and documentation. Some do this. Many don't.

What you're noticing when your manager disappears right when things get good isn't bad luck. It's the vendor's organizational structure working exactly as intended, which happens to be directly opposed to your interests. The system they've built optimizes for something else entirely: how they develop talent, how they allocate experienced managers, maybe how they respond to churn risk. Your smooth operation just wasn't on the list.

The weird part is how normal everyone acts about it. The outgoing manager says they'll make a great introduction. The new manager promises continuity. You're supposed to feel good about the transition, like it's no big deal. And from a certain angle it isn't. From yours, you just lost institutional knowledge and have to rebuild trust from scratch. Those are different things entirely.
