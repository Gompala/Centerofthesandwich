The best service teams stay focused on what their people are actually trying to accomplish for customers, not just how much time agents spend doing it.

There's something deeply human about wanting to measure things. It makes sense. When you're responsible for making sure a service runs well, tracking how your team spends their time feels like the obvious lever to pull. You can see it, quantify it, improve it. The metrics sit right there on your dashboard, clean and clear.

But here's what's interesting: organizations that get really good at service delivery tend to flip that priority around. They still care about efficiency, absolutely. But they've discovered that obsessing over agent activity metrics—how long calls take, how many tickets get closed, utilization rates—can actually create distance between the work and what the customer needs.

When the focus becomes tracking how agents spend their minutes, something shifts. The incentives start pointing inward. An agent might rush through a conversation to hit a time target, or close a ticket before it's really resolved, or avoid taking the complex questions that need real thinking. These aren't character flaws. They're just people responding to what they're being measured on.

The teams that deliver better service do something different. They measure whether customers actually got what they needed. They ask whether the person who took the ticket understood the full context. They check in on whether problems stay solved. They care about whether their agents have the information and tools to actually help someone, not just process them.

This shift changes how you support your team. Instead of hunting for efficiency gains, you start asking: What's stopping someone from doing great work? What information do they need? What decisions are we forcing them to make with incomplete context? Can we design this process so the right answer is also the easy answer?

The beautiful part is that this usually makes things faster anyway. When you remove friction from the actual service delivery, efficiency comes along naturally. When people aren't fighting against the system to do good work, they do it better and quicker.

It's worth reflecting on where your organization sits with this. What's getting measured in your service operations? What would change if you flipped the question from 'how do we track what people are doing' to 'how do we make it easy for people to do what customers need'? Those are very different questions, and they point toward very different kinds of teams.

The good news is that shift doesn't require a big rebuild. It just requires paying attention to where your focus actually goes.
