Office space planning works best when the people who manage the digital experience have a real seat at the table.

There's something satisfying about watching different parts of an organization discover they actually need each other. For years, real estate teams and digital workplace teams operated in parallel universes, each optimizing for their own world. Real estate focused on square footage, utilization rates, and lease terms. Digital workplace teams managed the networks, devices, and services that made work possible. Then something shifted. Organizations started asking a more interesting question: what does a workplace actually need to feel like a workplace?

That's where digital workplace teams become essential to space planning conversations. Not as an afterthought, not as people who show up to review the AV specs. From the very beginning.

When digital workplace teams are involved early, they bring something valuable to the conversation that nobody else naturally thinks about. They understand the rhythm of how work actually happens. They know which collaboration tools people reach for first. They can tell you where the network needs to be strongest, not just geographically but functionally. They understand the difference between a space designed for video calls and a space designed for hybrid work where half the room is remote and half is in person. They know that a beautifully appointed collaboration area doesn't work if the bandwidth can't handle it, or if people don't know the space exists, or if booking it is harder than just staying at their desks.

But it goes deeper than just technical requirements. Digital workplace teams understand employee experience in a way that connects the physical and digital sides. They see the whole picture. They know that a flexible work environment only feels flexible if people can actually move around without losing their connection. They understand that a new office layout only succeeds if people know how to navigate it, how to use it, what's available to them. That's communication work. That's change management. That's all part of digital workplace thinking.

When real estate, facilities, IT, and employee experience teams plan together from the start, something better emerges. The office becomes a place where the tools people need are actually there when they need them. Where the technology fades into the background and just works. Where coming to the office feels purposeful because the space is designed to support what people actually do.

It's collaborative work at its best. Different perspectives, different expertise, all aimed at one shared goal: making the workplace work for the people who use it. That's worth thinking about, and worth getting right.
