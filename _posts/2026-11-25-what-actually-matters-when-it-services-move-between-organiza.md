Service transitions during mergers and divestitures are genuinely interesting moments in how organizations work. They're moments when the often-invisible infrastructure of a digital workplace suddenly becomes very visible, and people across the organization start paying real attention to things that usually just work in the background.

There's something clarifying about these transitions. When two organizations are coming together, or when one is splitting apart, the conversations that happen in those digital workplace and IT service management meetings start from a different place than they usually do. Everyone suddenly has to articulate what they actually need. Not what they've always had, not what their system does, but what people need to accomplish their work.

The organizations that handle these transitions well seem to do something subtle differently. They don't start by inventorying what technology exists or which systems are 'better.' They start by thinking about the actual workflows. What does the finance team need to close the books? What does the sales team need to manage their pipeline? What does HR need to onboard people? Then they work backward to figure out which services and systems make that possible.

This reframing is powerful because it puts the person doing the work at the center. Not the technology, not the organizational structure, not the contracts with vendors. The person who needs to get something done in their day.

It also creates natural conversations about simplification. When you're asking 'what do people actually need to do this job,' sometimes you discover that you've been running three tools that do the same thing. Sometimes you realize a process that felt essential is actually optional. These are good conversations to have, and transitions give you permission to have them.

There's real craft in managing service transitions well. The people doing this work are thinking about communication cadences, about how to maintain business continuity while systems are moving, about how to support people through change without overwhelming them. They're thinking about the moments when someone is going to try to do something and something will work differently than it did before, and how to help them through that moment.

It requires holding a lot of complexity and detail in your head while never losing sight of the human experience on the other end. It requires genuine collaboration between technical teams, service management teams, business leaders, and the people whose work is being supported.

These transitions are also opportunities. They're moments when you can rebuild relationships between IT and the rest of the organization on a foundation of clarity and genuine service. They're moments when you can establish better ways of working and communicating that stick around long after the transition is done.
