Your manager probably understands more than you think — and wants to understand even more.

There's something genuinely interesting happening in organizations right now. Managers across digital workplace functions are becoming more curious about what their teams actually need, not in the abstract sense of 'what does our workforce need,' but in the real, everyday sense of 'what would make this person's job more interesting and more doable.'

Employees in digital workplace roles tend to wish their managers understood that what looks like a simple request from the business is often the tip of something more complex. A request to 'improve our intranet experience' might actually contain five different needs living under one umbrella — some about discoverability, some about trust, some about belonging. The person who made the request might not even know they're asking for five things. Your team does. Good managers increasingly want to understand that nuance, because it actually helps them lead better.

There's also something worth thinking about regarding visibility. People in digital workplace roles often work in the spaces between things — between what employees need and what the organization can provide, between what's technically possible and what's actually useful. This work is genuinely valuable, but it can feel invisible because it's happening in service to something else. When managers develop curiosity about how their teams spend their time, when they actually want to understand the thinking behind a decision or a project, something shifts. The work becomes real to them in a different way.

Managers also tend to wish they better understood how much their teams care about the actual experience employees have. It's not cynicism or martyrdom. People who work in digital workplace functions tend to genuinely want the humans in their organization to have a smoother day, to find what they need more easily, to feel like someone thought about them when designing a system or a process. When managers recognize that this care is real and valuable, they make different choices about resourcing, about priority, about what they push back on.

And perhaps most importantly, managers increasingly want to understand that their teams are thinking about the future. Not in a speculative, 'what might happen' way, but in a thoughtful, 'how do we build for what's coming' way. People in these roles are genuinely interested in whether the choices made today will still serve people well in a year, in five years.

The good news is that managers are leaning in. They're asking better questions. They're creating more space for their teams to explain their thinking. They're showing up to understand, not just to oversee. That's worth noticing and worth building on.
