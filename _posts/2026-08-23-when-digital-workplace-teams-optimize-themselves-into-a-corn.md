There's a particular moment in a digital workplace program where something shifts. The team has stabilized the infrastructure. Teams is working fine. SharePoint isn't on fire. The service desk is handling tickets. And suddenly the conversation changes. It's no longer about making anything better. It's about making it cheaper.

This is where things get interesting, and not in a good way.

The pivot to cost management isn't wrong on its surface. Every organization should care about efficiency. Waste is real. Unnecessary licenses, underused tools, bloated processes—these things exist and they matter. The problem isn't that someone is paying attention to the numbers. The problem is when that becomes the entire strategic conversation.

What happens next is predictable. The team starts optimizing for metrics that are easy to measure. License consumption goes down because you're consolidating tools or pushing people off expensive platforms. Ticket volume drops because you've made it harder to submit tickets. Operational expenses decline because you're doing less. These are clean, straightforward wins. They look great in a steering committee.

Meanwhile, something else is happening that doesn't show up in those metrics. People are working around the system because the system no longer works for them. Someone's team needs a collaboration space but it's not in the approved tool set, so they create an unapproved one. A department needs reporting that the digital workplace doesn't provide, so they build it themselves. An employee's workflow involves a tool you removed, so they figure out a clunky workaround.

You've reduced cost. What you've actually done is distribute it. You've moved expenses from the digital workplace budget into individual department budgets, hidden IT sprawl, and guaranteed that someone's productivity is taking a hit. The system looks good on the balance sheet. It's worse for the people using it.

The other thing that happens is the employee experience becomes an afterthought. Not intentionally. It's just not what you're optimizing for anymore. When you're focused on cost, every new request becomes a conversation about whether you can afford it, not whether it's valuable. Can we upgrade the video conferencing experience? That costs money, so probably not. Could we improve how onboarding works? That's nice, but it's not on the roadmap.

Then there's the customer piece, the external stakeholder. Some organizations have customers actually using their digital workplace—partners who need to collaborate, clients who access internal systems, vendors who need to work alongside the team. When you're optimizing for cost instead of service, these relationships feel it. Response times get longer. Feature requests go unanswered. Problems take longer to solve because you're running lean.

The tricky part is that cost cutting looks responsible. It feels like good management. You're being careful with money, watching the bottom line, not wasting resources. There's a real virtue in that. But it stops being virtuous the moment it becomes a constraint on everything else. When cost is the primary variable, experience becomes secondary, and that's when you end up with a digital workplace that technically works but nobody particularly enjoys using.

The organizations that figure this out tend to do something different. They set a baseline for cost management, sure. But then they ask a different question: given that constraint, what actually matters to the people using this system? What would make their work better? What's the problem we're trying to solve? Those questions lead to different priorities. Sometimes they cost less. Sometimes they cost more. But they're aligned with something other than just spending less money.
