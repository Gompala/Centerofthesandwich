A few years ago, the typical IT department looked like a kitchen drawer where someone just kept adding utensils without throwing any away. You'd have point solutions for everything, each vendor sending their own invoices, each requiring separate integrations, each needing its own admin access. It worked, in the way that a kitchen drawer works when you can still close it.

That's changing, and it's not because IT teams suddenly got organized. It's because the math stopped working.

The shift is subtle but real. Instead of asking "what's the best tool for X problem," teams are asking "can our existing vendor solve this, or is there something that solves both this and Y." It's a different question entirely. And it's driving conversations that weren't happening two years ago.

Part of it is just friction. Managing fifteen vendors means fifteen contracts, fifteen renewal dates, fifteen different support portals, fifteen different ways of doing basically the same thing. Someone has to remember which vendor owns which piece of your infrastructure. Someone has to track who has access to what. Someone has to negotiate with all of them. When budgets get tight, that friction becomes impossible to ignore.

But there's something else too. The vendors themselves are making consolidation easier. The big cloud providers have gotten much better at bundling services. Some of the enterprise software companies have been on buying sprees, folding smaller specialists into their platforms. A vendor that did one thing three years ago might now do three things reasonably well. That creates an actual option that didn't exist before.

There's also a real integration story here that people underestimate. When you're working with five different vendors, the connective tissue between them is fragile. APIs break, features don't quite sync, data lives in multiple places. It's not flashy but it's expensive. You're maintaining custom integrations, writing workarounds, and generally spending energy on making things talk to each other that should just work. Consolidation fixes that problem in a way that's hard to overstate.

The tradeoff is obvious. You're trading optionality for simplicity. You might not be getting the very best tool for every single job. You're betting that 85 percent of the way there with one vendor is better than 100 percent with five. That's a real bet, and it doesn't work for every situation.

But here's what makes it strategic rather than just a cost-cutting move: it changes how IT teams think about their role. When you have deep relationships with fewer vendors, you can actually influence their roadmap. You can push back on direction. You have leverage. You also have someone to call when things break at 2 AM who actually cares about your business. That relationship value is real, even if it's hard to quantify.

It also frees up attention for the stuff that actually matters. Instead of managing vendors, teams can focus on what those vendors are enabling. Infrastructure, security, user experience, innovation. The work that's actually strategic instead of the work that's just keeping the lights on.

The vendors who are winning right now are the ones who understand this aren't actually customers choosing simplicity over capability. They're choosing simplicity so they can focus on capability somewhere else. The vendors who can deliver enough breadth while staying deep on what matters are the ones who'll benefit most from this shift.
