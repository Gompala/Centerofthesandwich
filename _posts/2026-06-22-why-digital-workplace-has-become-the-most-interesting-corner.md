There's this thing that happens when you've been around tech long enough. You stop chasing the shiny new platforms and start noticing where actual energy is flowing. Right now, that energy is in the digital workplace.

Not in the headline-grabbing way. You're not seeing venture capital feeding digital workplace initiatives like they do with AI or cloud infrastructure. But something genuinely interesting is happening in how organizations think about where work happens, how people actually collaborate, and what role technology plays in that.

The shift is subtle enough that it's easy to miss if you're not paying attention. For decades, the digital workplace was basically treated as a checkbox. You had email, maybe a shared drive, some kind of messaging system. The assumption was that tools were tools, and people would just figure out how to use them.

What's changed is that this assumption broke. Visibly. The remote work surge forced it into the open, but the real story is what happened after. Organizations realized they couldn't just hand people a laptop and call it infrastructure. The collaboration patterns looked completely different when your team was distributed. The information that used to flow through hallways now had to be deliberately designed into systems. The spontaneous mentoring that happened by proximity had to be reimagined.

This opened up questions nobody was really asking before. How do new people actually get onboarded when they've never been in the office? Where does corporate knowledge actually live, and how fragile is that? What's the difference between having a tool and having a place where people can actually work together? Can you even measure that?

What's making this space interesting right now is that people are starting to answer these questions with real thoughtfulness instead of tech-flavored guesses. There's work happening around asynchronous communication patterns that goes way beyond "check your messages less often." There's genuine thinking about how to build systems where context doesn't evaporate the moment someone goes on vacation. How to structure information in a way that serves both the person who needs it today and the person who'll need it six months from now.

There's also this fascinating tension playing out. On one side, you've got the "let's just use more tools" approach. We'll solve the collaboration problem with another platform, another integration, another dashboard. On the other side, you're seeing teams step back and ask whether the real problem is actually too many tools creating friction instead of flow. That's not a small question. That's the whole game.

The people getting this right tend to share something in common. They're not betting everything on technology solving a human problem. They're using technology as part of understanding a human problem. They're asking service desk teams how they actually spend their time. They're watching how information moves. They're noticing which tools people spontaneously use versus which ones get abandoned. And they're willing to say "maybe we don't need another tool here."

There's also something refreshing about working in digital workplace right now because the stakes feel honest. You're not trying to decide whether to bet the company on some emerging technology that might be gone in three years. You're trying to figure out how to make the everyday experience of doing work a little bit better. How to help someone find what they need when they need it. How to make sure people aren't stuck in nineteen different applications just to get their job done. How to make sure the Friday afternoon question gets answered even if the person who knows the answer is three time zones away and offline.

It's not sexy work. It doesn't generate the same kind of attention as AI or quantum computing. But here's what's interesting: this is where you're actually affecting how millions of people spend eight hours of their day. Every bit of friction you reduce, every bottleneck you eliminate, every moment you give someone back, that compounds.

The digital workplace space right now also feels less crowded by ego. There's less of the "my approach is the only right approach" energy. More of a genuine "here's what we tried, here's what worked, here's what surprised us" conversation happening. People are actually learning from each other instead of competing to be the smartest person in the room.

If you're in tech and you're looking for somewhere that feels like there's actual work to do, actual problems to solve, and actual room for thoughtful solutions, the digital workplace is worth paying attention to. It's not going to make you famous. But it might make you useful. And after thirty years, I've noticed that useful tends to be more sustainable than famous anyway.
