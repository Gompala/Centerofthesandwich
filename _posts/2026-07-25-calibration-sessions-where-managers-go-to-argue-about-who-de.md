A calibration session is this strange ritual that happens once or twice a year in most organizations with enough headcount to need structure. A bunch of managers get in a room (or a Zoom, which somehow makes it weirder) and they spend hours comparing notes on their employees. Who got what rating. Who should get promoted. Who should get the bigger raise. It's like a bizarre hybrid of performance review and fantasy football draft, except the stakes are real people's careers and compensation.

The stated purpose is noble enough: make sure everyone's being evaluated fairly, prevent one manager from being a pushover while another is a tyrant, ensure consistency across the organization. In theory, this solves a real problem. In practice, you end up with something that feels less like quality control and more like organized negotiation theater.

What's genuinely strange about calibration sessions is how much they reveal about how organizations actually work versus how they say they work. Everyone walks in with their own agenda. The finance manager thinks their team is crushing it and deserves more budget. The product manager thinks the engineers are always overrated. Someone's always fighting for their high performer to get the promotion that should've gone to someone else. Someone's always defending why their underperformer should get another chance. It's diplomatic posturing with spreadsheets.

The real tell is that these sessions exist at all. If performance management actually worked the way the handbooks describe it, you wouldn't need a separate meeting to fix all the inconsistencies that happened in the individual reviews. You'd need calibration sessions the way you'd need a spelling check for a spell-checked document. The fact that they're essential means the individual review process is producing chaos that requires a separate governance layer to clean up. That's not quality assurance. That's damage control with a strategic name.

There's also something almost absurd about the format itself. You've got people who spend maybe a few hours a week thinking about any given employee having to defend their assessment to people who think about that employee even less. The manager who sits next to someone knows them. The person in the room from another department has maybe exchanged Slack messages with them. Yet somehow both opinions are supposed to carry equal weight when deciding whether that person gets a four or a five on their rating scale.

The meetings tend to follow predictable patterns. Someone makes a case. Someone else challenges it. There's discussion that sounds like deliberation but often amounts to whoever speaks most confidently getting their way. Then everyone moves on to the next person. By the end of the day, you've collectively decided the compensation and growth paths for dozens or hundreds of people in a process that feels less rigorous than most hiring decisions.

What's interesting is that people keep doing this. Organizations keep scheduling them. Managers keep preparing for them. Nobody seems to think it's as weird as it actually is. Maybe it's just become so normalized that it doesn't register as strange anymore. Or maybe the alternative, letting individual managers operate in complete isolation, seems worse enough that the weirdness is considered acceptable overhead.

Calibration sessions exist because organizations got big enough that they needed to coordinate judgment at scale, and nobody's figured out a less awkward way to do it. So here we are, once or twice a year, watching managers essentially lobby for their teams in a conference room. It works well enough. It's just strange that this is how we decided to solve the consistency problem.
