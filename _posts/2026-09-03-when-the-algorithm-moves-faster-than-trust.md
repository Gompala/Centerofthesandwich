There's a moment happening in IT departments right now that doesn't get talked about much. It's the split second after an AI system recommends something, and before anyone acts on it. Because the recommendation is usually good. Sometimes it's better than what a human would have picked. But there's this gap between "the system says do this" and "we're actually comfortable doing this," and that gap is where a lot of real work happens.

Consider a straightforward scenario: cloud migration decisions. An AI analysis can scan your infrastructure, compare costs across regions and providers, factor in latency requirements, and surface the optimal path in minutes. It's not guessing. It's reading thousands of data points and applying logic humans can't process that quickly. The thing is, no organization I've talked to just takes that recommendation and runs with it. Someone still has to validate it. Someone still has to think about things the algorithm might not have weighted correctly, like the team in one office that has terrible internet or the vendor relationship that actually matters more than the spreadsheet suggests.

This isn't a failure of the AI. It's actually where human judgment stays irreplaceable. But it creates this weird tension where the fastest path forward (which the AI just gave you) isn't the path that feels safe enough to take. So you end up slower than you would have been without the recommendation in the first place, because now you have to scrutinize a suggestion instead of just making a decision from first principles.

The organizations handling this best aren't the ones trying to move faster. They're the ones who've decided what human judgment actually needs to check. They've drawn a line: these decisions are safe to accelerate (routine patch deployments, routine access provisioning), and these ones need a person in the loop regardless of how confident the AI is (licensing models, security policies, infrastructure changes that affect multiple teams). That sounds obvious until you realize how much time gets spent arguing about where those lines should be, and how often they shift when someone gets burned by a decision that moved too fast.

What's interesting is how this changes what IT leaders actually need from their people. The bottleneck isn't analysis anymore. It's judgment. It's the ability to ask good questions about why an algorithm chose what it chose, and whether the thing it's optimizing for is actually what the organization should be optimizing for. That's a different skill than it used to be. A lot of IT organizations are still structured around people who are good at the analysis part, not the critique part.

There's also something quieter happening, which is that people are starting to feel like they're either in the way or indispensable, with no middle ground. If the AI is right most of the time, why are you there? If it's wrong often enough to need you checking it, why isn't someone fixing the AI? Neither of those framings is quite right, but they're the ones floating around, and they shape how people think about their own role.

The tension between speed and judgment isn't really about speed and judgment. It's about which decisions are safe to optimize for speed and which ones need to stay slow because the cost of being wrong is too high. That calculus is different in every organization. The ones figuring it out aren't moving faster. They're just being honest about why they're not.
