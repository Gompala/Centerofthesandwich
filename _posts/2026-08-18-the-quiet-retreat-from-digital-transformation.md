Three years ago, every company with a budget was announcing a digital transformation initiative. The language was consistent: reimagine, future-proof, unlock potential, drive adoption. There were Gantt charts and change management frameworks and cross-functional steering committees. A lot of resources moved around. A lot of people got new job titles with the word "digital" in them.

Now something different is happening, and it's mostly happening in email threads and hallway conversations rather than in board presentations. Companies are scaling back these efforts, but they're doing it quietly, without the ceremonial shutdown that matches the launch.

It's not that digital transformation failed everywhere. Some organizations found real value in rethinking how teams work together, in modernizing their tech stack, in actually moving away from the seventeen different systems that had somehow accumulated. But plenty of others ran into the thing that always gets underestimated: the actual work of changing how people work.

Implementing a new collaboration platform like Teams is straightforward if you're just looking at deployment. Rolling out new workflows, actually getting people to adopt them instead of just layering them on top of existing patterns, dealing with the fact that some teams genuinely work better with different approaches—that's friction. That's the part that requires sustained attention and usually doesn't show up as a line item in the budget proposal.

There's also the basic problem of momentum. A digital transformation initiative sounds like something you drive toward, but what companies often discovered is that driving toward it requires continuous communication, consistent leadership attention, and clear benchmarks for what actually changes. When budget gets tight or leadership attention gets pulled elsewhere—which happens constantly in real organizations—these initiatives stop feeling urgent. They become background noise. People drift back to what they were doing before, except now they're technically supposed to be doing it in a new system.

Some of the pullback is also just pragmatism settling in. The companies that oversold what digital transformation would accomplish are now living with the gap between the promise and the reality. A better CRM system doesn't automatically improve customer relationships. A shiny new intranet doesn't magically improve internal communication if the underlying culture doesn't support it. These things are tools for already-functioning organizations, not solutions for broken ones.

What's interesting is how little anyone wants to talk about this. You don't see many articles about "the initiatives we're quietly winding down" or "where our transformation strategy missed the mark." Instead, you see companies either quietly reducing headcount on these teams, reallocating budget to other priorities, or just letting the momentum die without a formal sunset.

None of this means digital transformation is pointless. Organizations do need to think seriously about how technology shapes their work. But the confident, top-down, "we're doing this in 18 months" approach keeps running into the same wall: people don't change how they work because you told them to or because you bought them new software. They change when they see a reason to, when the friction is lower than their current approach, when their leader actually uses it and expects them to.

The companies that actually pulled off meaningful change were the ones that treated it less like a transformation campaign and more like ongoing work. Less announcement, more follow-through. Less promise, more evidence that things were actually different.

The retreat we're seeing now is partly companies admitting that the model didn't work. The ones doing it quietly are at least being honest about that, even if they're not saying it out loud.
