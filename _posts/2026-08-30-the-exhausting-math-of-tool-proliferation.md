There's a moment that happens in most organizations right around the time they've settled on their core platforms. Someone attends a conference. Someone reads an article. A team somewhere discovers that a specialized tool exists for their exact workflow, and it's genuinely better at that one thing than whatever they're currently using.

So they ask for it. And it makes sense to say yes. It's not expensive. It's just one more tool. It handles a specific job better than the general-purpose solution they have.

Then another team does the same thing. And another. And somewhere around tool number seven or eight, people stop noticing that they're having the same conversation across multiple platforms. A decision gets made in Teams, gets documented in SharePoint, gets tracked in one system, gets communicated through another. A team member needs to check four different places to understand the status of something that probably should have had one source of truth.

The cost isn't really the subscription fees. It's softer and harder to measure at the same time. It's the cognitive load of context switching. It's the way information fragments. It's how a project that should take twenty minutes now requires someone to pull details from three different systems and manually stitch them together. It's how onboarding becomes this exhausting tour of six separate login screens. It's the fact that when someone tries to search for something later, they can't remember which tool it lived in.

What makes this harder to fix is that nobody's wrong about the individual decisions. Each tool genuinely does solve something better than what came before. The problem is systemic, not local. From any single team's perspective, adding a specialized tool for their workflow is sensible. From the organization's perspective, you've created fragmentation.

The organizations that seem to handle this better aren't the ones with the most advanced toolsets. They're the ones that have been ruthlessly disciplined about consolidation. They've decided that a tool has to be genuinely exceptional at something before it breaks the integration of the whole system. They've accepted that sometimes ninety percent good across everything is better than ninety-five percent good in one place and seventy percent in another.

This requires a different kind of leadership thinking. It means sometimes telling teams they can't have the perfect tool because having fifteen different systems is worse than being slightly less efficient in any one of them. It means the people designing the digital workplace architecture have to think about the system as a whole, not just about what IT is purchasing.

It also means being honest about what problems you're actually trying to solve. A lot of tool sprawl happens because organizations are trying to solve people problems with software problems. Bad process doesn't get fixed by adding a new tracking system. Unclear communication doesn't get fixed by adding another chat channel. What it does do is create more places where things can get lost.

The weird thing about the modern digital workplace is that more capability often feels like less capability from a user perspective. Someone drowning in six tools doesn't feel more empowered than someone working with three well-integrated ones. They feel scattered.

The discipline that's actually valuable isn't keeping up with what's new. It's being selective about what gets added, and being willing to say that better is the enemy of good enough when it comes to maintaining coherence across your entire environment.
