There's this moment that happens in IT leadership where you realize two people at roughly the same level, with similar technical chops and comparable tenures, are suddenly on completely different trajectories. One's getting pulled into bigger conversations. The other's managing the same infrastructure they've been managing for three years.

It's not usually about technical depth. The person who gets stuck often knows their domain better. They can troubleshoot faster, they understand the legacy systems nobody else wants to touch, they're the person everyone calls at midnight. They're genuinely valuable.

But valuable and promotable are different animals.

The people who keep moving up tend to do something subtle that doesn't show up in performance reviews. They stop thinking of their job as solving the current problem and start thinking of it as solving the next problem. Not in a restless way, not in a "I want out of this role" way. More like they're genuinely curious about what comes after they fix the thing they're fixing.

This shows up in how they talk about their work. The person who's stuck talks about what they built, what they fixed, what they maintain. The person who's moving up talks about what changed because they built it. They connect the dots between their infrastructure decisions and business outcomes. They're not being asked to do this. They just notice the connection and can't help mentioning it.

They also seem weirdly comfortable admitting what they don't know. Not in a performative way, but in a genuinely curious way. They'll walk into a finance meeting about IT budget and ask actual questions about how the business prioritizes spending, not to challenge it but because they actually want to understand. Meanwhile, the person who's plateauing tends to stay in their lane, which feels safer but also makes them invisible to the parts of the organization where promotion decisions get made.

There's also something about how they treat people junior to them. The people who get promoted early seem to have this reflex to explain not just what to do but why they're doing it that way. They hand off problems in a way that builds capacity in other people, which means they're not indispensable for day-to-day operations. They're free to think about larger things. The other way around, where you become the bottleneck because you're the only one who understands the critical systems, actually works against you in the long run. It makes you valuable in your current role but unpromotable out of it.

The weirdest part is that the people who get promoted faster don't seem like they're playing a game. They're not strategic in a calculated way. They just seem more interested in how their work fits into the bigger picture, more willing to learn adjacent things, more comfortable sitting with people outside their function. They read like people who want to be useful in larger ways, not people who want to climb.

Which might be the whole thing right there. The ones who get stuck are often the ones treating promotion like the goal. The ones who keep moving are treating it like what naturally happens when you become genuinely useful in contexts beyond your current one.

The gap isn't talent. It's perspective.
