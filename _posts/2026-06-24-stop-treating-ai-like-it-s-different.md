There's this moment happening right now where people are split on AI. Half the office is already using it to cut their email time in half. The other half is treating it like some kind of threat that needs to be managed carefully. Both reactions feel kind of overblown.

The thing is, this isn't really unprecedented. It's just the next thing.

## We've Always Done This

Think about how everyone reacted when email became standard. Suddenly you had to answer messages that used to arrive on paper. That was disruptive. People had opinions about it. Some folks loved the speed, some hated the constant interruption. Now? Nobody's debating whether email is worth it. It's just part of how work happens.

Automation lines in manufacturing did the same thing. Showed up, changed everything, sparked all kinds of conversations about what it meant for workers and processes. Eventually everyone figured out how to work alongside them, and suddenly you're building better products faster. The technology didn't disappear because people were nervous about it. It just became normal.

The internet was the big one though. That was genuinely disruptive. Totally rewired how information moved, how teams could be distributed, what was even possible. And yet here we are, building entire businesses around it now without thinking twice.

## The Opportunity Part

What's interesting about AI right now is that it's not some distant thing that'll eventually roll out to everyone. It's already available if you want to use it. That's different from automation or the internet, where there was usually some infrastructure you had to wait for or an organization making decisions for you.

So there's this window where you can actually figure out what works for your specific situation, at your pace. Someone's probably already using it to draft meeting notes so they can actually pay attention instead of typing. Someone else is using it to sort through documentation that would've taken an afternoon. None of that requires waiting for permission or a company rollout plan.

The streamlining angle is real too. Not in a way that's creepy or replacing people, but in the way that handles the stuff that's genuinely tedious. Formatting. Summarizing. Finding that one thing you remember reading three weeks ago. These are the things that eat time without actually being your job.

## It's Just Another Tool

The fear side makes sense on some level. New technology always comes with questions about what it means for how we work. Fair to think about that. But the way to deal with it isn't to wait and see what everyone else does. It's to just start using it and figure out what actually helps versus what's just novelty.

That's what people did with their first email account. Awkward at first, then it clicked, then it became invisible. Same thing happened when cloud storage went mainstream. Seems weird, doesn't fit your workflow, then suddenly you can't imagine not having it.

AI's going through that phase right now. It's still in the "some people think it's amazing and some people are skeptical" window. That window always closes eventually. The skeptics try it, either it clicks or it doesn't, and then it becomes another tool in the drawer.

## Just Use It

The honest part? There's no downside to trying. Play with a copilot for a week. See if it actually saves you time or if it just feels like extra work to use it. Maybe it's perfect for how you think and maybe it's not. That's the only way to actually know.

And yeah, there are legitimate conversations to have about how these tools work at scale, what they mean for how organizations operate, how we want them to fit into our culture. Those are real conversations. But those conversations can happen while people are also using them and figuring out what's actually useful.

The companies and teams that figure this out early aren't doing anything magical. They're just not waiting for the fear to resolve itself before they start experimenting. They're taking the same approach everyone took with the internet or automation or email. They're treating it like the next thing, because it is.

Everyone gets nervous about new tech. Then everyone gets used to it. This time is no different.
