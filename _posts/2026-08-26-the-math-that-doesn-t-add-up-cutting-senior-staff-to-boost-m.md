There's a pattern emerging in how some digital workplace service organizations are approaching their cost structure, and it's one worth paying attention to because the incentives look reasonable until they suddenly aren't.

The logic is straightforward: senior staff cost more. They command higher salaries, they expect more benefits, they take up valuable budget allocation. If you're running a services business where margins matter and contracts are competitive, the math pulls you toward one conclusion. Replace them with less expensive talent, invest in tooling and process to compensate, and watch your profit line improve.

On paper, this works. On paper, lots of things work.

What actually happens is messier. When you staff a service delivery team with primarily junior and mid-level talent, you're making a bet about complexity. You're betting that the problems your customers will face are predictable, that the environments are standard, that escalations will be manageable. Most of the time, one or all of those bets fail.

A team without seniority tends to get stuck on problems that experienced people would navigate past. They escalate differently. They don't know which corners can be cut and which ones can't. They make decisions that are technically defensible but operationally wrong. They need oversight they don't get, because you've also reduced the senior staff who would provide it.

The customer experience deteriorates in ways that aren't immediate or dramatic. Response times get longer. Issues take more back-and-forths to resolve. Changes that should take a week take three weeks because there's no one confident enough to approve them. The digital workplace environment that was running smoothly starts feeling sluggish and frustrating.

What's interesting is that the cost savings materialize faster than the service degradation. For the first six months or a year, the margin improvement looks real. The profit numbers are there. Then customers start mentioning it in conversations. Then they mention it in renewal negotiations. Then they start looking at alternatives.

The organizations that do this well enough to get away with it usually have something protecting them: a sticky contract, low switching costs, a captive market, or genuinely exceptional process discipline that compensates for the experience gap. Most don't have all of those things. Many don't have any of them.

There's also the internal experience to consider. Junior staff who are understaffed and unsupported burn out. The people who could have grown into senior roles often leave instead because they're not learning and they're drowning. The institutional knowledge that makes a service operation run smoothly leaks out the door.

So you end up in a situation where you've reduced your costs and reduced your ability to deliver at the same time. The margins look better in the short term, but you've made your service organization more fragile. You've reduced the shock absorption capacity. When something unusual happens, or when a customer actually needs more than the baseline, the whole system gets stressed.

The organizations getting this right tend to think about it differently. They optimize for retention and capability at the senior level, knowing that those people are what allow you to handle complexity without constantly escalating. They invest in tooling and process, but as additions to their team, not replacements for it. They accept that better margins come later, after they've built something customers actually want to keep.

It's not a novel insight. It's just one that the spreadsheet view of cost management makes easy to overlook.
