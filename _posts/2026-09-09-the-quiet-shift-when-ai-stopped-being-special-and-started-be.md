AI is settling into the background of how work actually gets done, and that's exactly when it starts mattering.

There's something worth noticing happening in digital workplaces right now. A few months ago, everyone was talking about artificial intelligence as this separate thing, this tool you had to think about and decide whether to use. You'd deliberate. You'd wonder if it was ready. You'd have meetings about whether your organization should adopt it.

Now it's just there.

Not in a creepy way, and not in a way that anyone had to pretend surprise about. It's more like how we stopped having long conversations about whether email belonged in the workplace. It belongs because it works, and because it solves problems people actually have.

What's genuinely interesting is how this is changing the shape of service delivery for the people managing digital experiences. When AI was the headline, it required explanation and intent and careful rollout. Now it's becoming part of how you naturally solve problems. Someone needs help troubleshooting their setup, and there's an AI-powered assistant in the knowledge base that understands the question in a human way. A team is drowning in routine requests, and they've woven intelligent automation into their workflow without needing it to be a Project. Someone's looking for information buried in documentation, and the search actually works.

The conversation has shifted from 'should we use this?' to 'how do we use this well?' That's a healthier place to be. It means the technology is actually useful enough that people stopped asking whether it was viable and started asking how to make it better.

What makes this moment genuinely good is that organizations are building this thoughtfully. There's real care going into how AI touches the employee experience. People are thinking about accuracy and reliability and what happens when something goes wrong. They're not treating it like magic. They're treating it like what it is: a capable tool that makes certain kinds of work easier, particularly the work that was never the interesting part anyway.

For anyone leading in digital workplace functions, this is worth staying curious about. Not because there's some emergency to respond to, but because the shape of work is genuinely evolving. The questions you're asking your teams aren't about whether AI is coming. They're about how you want it to work for the people who depend on your systems every day. How do you make things faster without making them worse? How do you handle the boring stuff so people can focus on what matters? How do you keep trust when you're adding new layers to how work happens?

Those are good questions. The fact that we're asking them now, without the hype, means we might actually get good answers.
